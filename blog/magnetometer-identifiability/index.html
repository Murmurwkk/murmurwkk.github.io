Why a Perfect Magnetometer Fit Says Nothing About Direction

Ellipsoid calibration fixes each reading's length, so any rotation of a valid calibration fits equally well. The rotation gauge, when planar motion starves heading, and a real column-order bug in the logs.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 9 个章节
#magnetometer#identifiability#sensor calibration#experiment audit

The standard recipe for magnetometer calibration goes like this. Wave the phone around, collect readings, fit an ellipsoid, then remove the offset and the direction-dependent stretch so that, in a constant field, every reading lands on the same sphere. When the residuals go to zero, you’re calibrated.

Except that condition only constrains the length of each vector. It says nothing about which way the sphere faces relative to the device’s axes. If CCC is a calibration matrix that satisfies it, then QCQCQC for any rotation QQQ gives exactly the same residuals. Ellipsoid fitting has a built-in rotational degree of freedom. Cover the entire sphere, push the fit error down to floating-point precision, and the magnitudes still can’t tell you which direction is right.

That’s a different problem from not having enough samples, and different again from reading the file wrong. This post separates all three. We’ll start by splitting “calibration” into the three questions it usually lumps together, derive the rotation gauge, see it on constructed data and then see what planar motion does to the fit. After that we’ll look at what extra information pins down axes and absolute yaw, and finish with two implementation bugs found in the historical pipeline, one of them in how real MAGU log lines were parsed.

Three questions people lump together

Let RWDR_{WD}RWD​ rotate device-frame vectors into the world frame. A convenient model is

y=ARWDTmW(p,t)+b+ϵ.y=A R_{WD}^{T}m_W(p,t)+b+\epsilon.y=ARWDT​mW​(p,t)+b+ϵ.

mWm_WmW​ is the world-frame magnetic field, which depends on position and time; bbb is an additive bias; AAA lumps together scale, non-orthogonality, linear distortion and axis relationships. The model only holds within its valid range. Device state changes, moving accessories or environmental changes can all invalidate a fixed A,bA,bA,b.

“Calibrating the magnetometer” can mean three distinct things:

  1. Magnitude and shape. After removing bias and direction-dependent stretch, readings in a constant field should lie near a sphere.
  2. Sensor-to-device axis alignment. Is a given vector on that sphere expressed in the same device frame the gyroscope and accelerometer use?
  3. Device-to-world orientation. How does the device frame relate to the chosen world reference? And “magnetic north,” “geographic north” and “the indoor map’s x-axis” aren’t the same reference either.

Getting the first right doesn’t solve the other two. And even a known axis alignment under one fixed mounting doesn’t guarantee the same field model carries over to an environment with magnetic disturbances.

Fundamentals: magnitude fixes the quadratic form, not the direction

Assume a locally constant field, normalized to length 1. Write y=Au+by=Au+by=Au+b with ∥u∥=1\|u\|=1∥u∥=1 and AAA invertible. Then

(y−b)TH(y−b)=1,H=A−TA−1.(y-b)^T H(y-b)=1, \qquad H=A^{-T}A^{-1}.(y−b)TH(y−b)=1,H=A−TA−1.

Fitting an ellipsoid estimates the center bbb and the symmetric positive-definite matrix HHH. Pick any CCC with CTC=HC^TC=HCTC=H. For every rotation QQQ,

∥QC(y−b)∥2=(y−b)TCTQTQC(y−b)=(y−b)TH(y−b).\|QC(y-b)\|^2 =(y-b)^TC^TQ^TQC(y-b) =(y-b)^TH(y-b).∥QC(y−b)∥2=(y−b)TCTQTQC(y−b)=(y−b)TH(y−b).

So CCC and QCQCQC explain every magnitude observation identically. Requiring a positive determinant to rule out reflections still leaves the full 3D rotation free. Choosing the symmetric square root C=H1/2C=H^{1/2}C=H1/2 is a unique numerical convention, but a unique convention isn’t a unique physical axis relationship.

The polar decomposition makes this even more explicit. For handedness-preserving AAA, write A=SUA=SUA=SU with SSS symmetric positive definite and UUU a rotation. Then H=S−2H=S^{-2}H=S−2, symmetric whitening gives C=S−1C=S^{-1}C=S−1, and

C(y−b)=S−1SUu=Uu.C(y-b)=S^{-1}SUu=Uu.C(y−b)=S−1SUu=Uu.

The stretch is gone and the unknown rotation UUU is left completely intact. Magnitude residuals can approach zero while every vector’s direction is systematically off by UUU.

If the true field strength is also unknown, absolute scale is one more convention: the unit sphere is a normalization and can’t recover a µT scale. And if the field strength varies along an indoor route, the assumption that all points come from one ellipsoid has to be re-checked. Absorbing field variation into AAA can produce a beautiful fit and destroy the physical interpretation at the same time.

Seeing it: three equally good calibrations

Let’s watch the gauge freedom happen. The constructed experiment uses 1000 Fibonacci sphere directions, noise-free and dimensionless, transformed by A=SRz(35∘)A=S R_z(35^\circ)A=SRz​(35∘) with

The fitter sees only the transformed yyy. It subtracts the point-set mean, solves an algebraic least squares over nine quadratic, cross and linear terms, recovers the center and quadratic form, and takes the positive-definite square root. The true uuu is used only afterwards to measure error, never as supervision.

When excitation is planar

The gauge freedom is there even with perfect data. Poor excitation adds a second, separate problem. A second construction also has 1000 points, but all true vectors lie on one horizontal circle, with the same A,bA,bA,b applied. An SVD of the centered nine-column design matrix gives numerical rank 9 for full-sphere input and 5 for planar input. The planar case’s sixth singular value is about 5.13×10−155.13\times10^{-15}5.13×10−15 against a largest of 65.42, which is floating-point noise.

This isn’t “1000 points isn’t enough.” Adding more points in the same plane can’t supply the missing third dimension of excitation. Noise can turn exact zero singular values into small positive ones, but that’s not a reliable constraint. Regularizing the inverse keeps the arithmetic running; the direction the regularizer picks should be labeled as prior, not counted as information from the data.

What it takes to pin down direction

If magnitudes can’t fix direction, what can? It depends on which of the three questions you’re asking.

Axis alignment: inertial data

Kok and Schön’s Magnetometer calibration using inertial sensors (IEEE Sensors Journal, 2016, 16(14):5679–5689) uses inertial data for axis alignment and offline maximum-likelihood calibration, with ellipsoid fitting only as initialization. Their experiments require slow rotations and validate with relative 90° turns of a rectangular block, which isn’t an absolute geographic heading reference (paper v3 and experiments). I’m citing its construction of extra constraints, not reproducing its algorithm or hardware experiments.

The general rule: unit-sphere residuals check the shape of the observations. Checking axis alignment needs motion constraints consistent with an inertial reference. Checking absolute device orientation needs a direction reference that doesn’t share its answer with the magnetometer under test. If training and checking both come from one magnetometer fit, no residual is small enough to substitute for that last one.

Absolute yaw: you need a horizontal reference

Even with AAA and bbb perfectly known, there’s a second gauge at the world level. For any rotation GGG about the world vertical, set

RWD′=GRWD,mW′=GmW.R'_{WD}=G R_{WD},\qquad m'_W=Gm_W.RWD′​=GRWD​,mW′​=GmW​.

Then RWD′TmW′=RWDTmWR_{WD}'^{T}m'_W=R_{WD}^{T}m_WRWD′T​mW′​=RWDT​mW​: the magnetic reading is unchanged, and so is gravity. So in a model with no known horizontal reference, the device’s absolute yaw and the horizontal orientation of the field can’t be determined separately.

Gyroscopes provide relative rotation. They constrain attitude change over time and sensor axis relationships, but integrating them doesn’t produce geographic north. Defining some magnetic direction as the world x-axis is a choice of convention; proving it’s the building map’s x-axis needs an external direction relationship. And the walking direction isn’t device yaw either: someone walking in a straight line can hold the phone sideways, or rotate it.

How much a magnetometer tells you about yaw

Now grant much stronger assumptions: the local field vector is known, the device’s tilt relative to gravity (roll/pitch) is known, only a small yaw perturbation remains, and each axis has independent zero-mean Gaussian noise of variance σ2\sigma^2σ2. With eze_zez​ the vertical unit vector in the device frame and uuu the predicted device-frame field, the local Jacobian and Fisher information are

Hψ=−ez×u,Iψ=HψT(σ2I)−1Hψ=∥uh∥2σ2.H_\psi=-e_z\times u, \qquad \mathcal I_\psi =H_\psi^T(\sigma^2I)^{-1}H_\psi =\frac{\|u_h\|^2}{\sigma^2}.Hψ​=−ez​×u,Iψ​=HψT​(σ2I)−1Hψ​=σ2∥uh​∥2​.

The sign depends on rotation convention; the information doesn’t. Only uhu_huh​, the horizontal component, appears. However strong the field, if it’s nearly vertical, small yaw changes barely affect the reading, and with zero horizontal component the yaw information is exactly zero.

The right panel of Figure 3 uses a demo value of σ=1\sigma=1σ=1 µT. Horizontal components of 5 and 20 µT give information of 25 and 400 rad⁻². Under the usual regularity conditions, the single-sample Cramér–Rao variance bound for a local unbiased estimator is 1/Iψ1/\mathcal I_\psi1/Iψ​, i.e. standard-deviation lower bounds of about 11.46° and 2.86°. These are theoretical quantities under a conditional model, not guarantees for arbitrary biased estimators and not measurements of two phones; at low SNR, the local bound doesn’t describe the real circular error distribution either. And this formula assumes a known field, so it can’t be used to quietly make the unknown-field problem from the previous section disappear.

If frames share calibration error or environmental change, information doesn’t simply multiply by the number of samples. Correlated error belongs in the joint covariance, the same issue discussed in how common-source observations update the state.

Short-window dynamics

Short-term dynamics can add constraints too, at the cost of modeling environmental change. For a debiased, axis-aligned vector u=RTm(p,t)u=R^Tm(p,t)u=RTm(p,t),

u˙=−ωD×u+RT∇m p˙+RT∂m∂t.\dot u=-\omega_D\times u +R^T\nabla m\,\dot p +R^T\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}.u˙=−ωD​×u+RT∇mp˙​+RT∂t∂m​.

The field vector can change because the device rotates, because it moves through a spatial gradient, or because the field itself changes over time. Build the residual r=u˙+ωgyro×ur=\dot u+\omega_{gyro}\times ur=u˙+ωgyro​×u and it contains gyro bias and the last two terms. Without constraints on local field stability, these explanations can stand in for each other.

A fixed additive bias cancels in a plain difference, but plugging raw y=u+by=u+by=u+b into the whole residual leaves an ωgyro×b\omega_{gyro}\times bωgyro​×b term. Per-sample differencing also amplifies high-frequency noise. So “we used differences” doesn’t skip the bias and axis checks, and the mixed residual isn’t pure gyro error.

Attitude invariants have limits too. When calibration and gravity are trustworthy, magnitude, vertical projection and horizontal magnitude are good field-consistency checks. They’re insensitive to pure yaw, which is precisely why they can’t recover yaw. Passing a consistency check means a hypothesis survives, not that other explanations are ruled out.

To sum up this section: magnitude fitting recovers the ellipsoid’s quadratic form; inertial motion constraints connect the sensor axes one step further; absolute heading needs a known horizontal reference. Three goals, three kinds of information. You can’t sign off on all three with one unit-sphere residual plot.

Two implementation bugs worth knowing about

The rest of the post is about the historical pipeline, where two concrete bugs turned up.

The constant term after shifting an ellipsoid

An older 72-log calibration report recorded an error in recovering the ellipsoid’s constant term. I only recomputed its algebraic counterexample here, not the report’s 72 logs.

In coordinates xxx with the fitting origin already subtracted, the fit is

xTQx+2vTx=1,c=−Q−1v.x^TQx+2v^Tx=1, \qquad c=-Q^{-1}v.xTQx+2vTx=1,c=−Q−1v.

Completing the square should give

(x−c)TQ(x−c)=1−vTc.(x-c)^TQ(x-c)=1-v^Tc.(x−c)TQ(x−c)=1−vTc.

Here ccc must still be in the local fitting coordinates; a center already mapped back to raw coordinates can’t be plugged into this constant. Take (x−1)2=4(x-1)^2=4(x−1)2=4, written as x2/3−2x/3=1x^2/3-2x/3=1x2/3−2x/3=1: then Q=1/3Q=1/3Q=1/3, v=−1/3v=-1/3v=−1/3 and c=1c=1c=1. The correct right-hand side is 4/34/34/3, recovering radius 2. Flip the sign to 1+vTc1+v^Tc1+vTc and you recover radius 2\sqrt{2}2​.

One sign changes the recovered scale even though the quadratic fit itself didn’t fail. The fitting code explicitly checks rank, positive definiteness, recovered center and error; it’s still just an implementation for a noise-free counterexample, not a production calibrator for robust natural data.

MAGU’s interleaved columns

Field semantics were checked on IPIN training log Trial21 / session01 / SG24, after verifying the raw file’s full SHA-256. The two kinds of magnetometer lines in the file have different field orders:

MAGN;AppTimestamp;SensorTimestamp;Mag_X;Mag_Y;Mag_Z;Accuracy
MAGU;AppTimestamp;SensorTimestamp;Mag_X;bias_X;Mag_Y;bias_Y;Mag_Z;bias_Z;Accuracy

MAGU interleaves field and bias per axis. For a semicolon-split array p with p[0] holding the record type, the correct read is:

field = np.array([float(p[3]), float(p[5]), float(p[7])])
bias  = np.array([float(p[4]), float(p[6]), float(p[8])])
device_debiased = field - bias

That report says an even earlier analysis read MAGU’s field as p[3:6] and bias as p[6:9], which is the natural guess if you assume MAGN’s layout. I didn’t recover or run that old script; I reproduced the described formula on raw lines. The current repository’s src/ipin/frontends/magnetic/raw.py handles MAGN, so this historical problem doesn’t apply to that module.

Line 61780 of the raw log gives a model-free arithmetic counterexample, in µT:

It’s easy to confuse the Android API with the on-disk format here. The official uncalibrated magnetometer interface puts the three field values and three biases in separate array positions, while this capture app chose per-axis interleaving; a file reader must follow the file’s own header. The official docs also note that “uncalibrated” doesn’t mean unprocessed: the output may still include factory calibration and temperature compensation, and the regular magnetic field output is roughly the uncalibrated field minus the estimated bias (Android: Position sensors).

Checking all of the first 256 lines, not just one

To avoid cherry-picking a good line, I took the first 256 MAGU lines in the file, covering AppTimestamp 0.024–5.114 s, and for each found the MAGN line with exactly the same SensorTimestamp. All 256 matched, with no nearest-neighbor pairing, interpolation or time shifting.

That cross-check has limits. Both streams come from the same device and may share the same calibration, so this is not two independent instruments certifying each other, and the small differences can’t be called the magnetometer’s noise standard deviation. These 256 points weren’t used to fit a natural-data ellipsoid or to estimate heading accuracy.

The timestamps need the same restraint. Lines 61780 and 61786 have SensorTimestamps of 25950.082 and 25950.102 s, yet identical text in all six field and bias values. That’s enough to refute “different timestamps always mean different values.” But two lines can’t say whether the cause is sample-and-hold, quantization or a stable input, and they don’t justify a new de-duplication policy for the whole log. Deleting independent samples that happen to share values would also change the noise statistics.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows, exactly, that ellipsoid calibration leaves a full 3D rotation free, and on constructed data that three calibrations with identical residuals can differ by 60° in direction.
  • It shows that planar excitation drops the design-matrix rank from 9 to 5 regardless of sample count, and that local yaw information depends only on the horizontal field component.
  • It shows, on a real log, that the MAGU column-order bug alone produces a 381 µT magnitude, and that correctly parsed MAGU matches same-timestamp MAGN to 0.008160 µT RMSE over 256 lines.
  • It doesn’t calibrate any natural-data ellipsoid or measure heading accuracy; the Fisher-information numbers are conditional theory, not phone measurements.
  • It doesn’t rerun the historical 72-log, 316-revisit comparison from that older report.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. “Magnetometer calibration” is three problems: magnitude/shape, sensor-to-device axes, and device-to-world orientation. Ellipsoid fitting solves only the first, and it provably leaves a rotation free. Planar motion removes even part of the first. Axes need inertial constraints, absolute yaw needs an independent horizontal reference, and how much yaw you can learn at all depends on the horizontal field strength. Underneath all of that, a parser that assumes the wrong column order can manufacture a field anomaly out of nothing.

A perfect sphere tells you the length of every vector and nothing about where any of them point.

Evaluating real gains would need those historical identities plus an independent direction reference. Similar magnitudes on repeated visits to one place support repeatable field features, but they don’t certify device heading.

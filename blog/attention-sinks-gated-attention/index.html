How an Attention Head Says Nothing: Sinks and Output Gates

Softmax makes every head output a weighted average, so a head can't scale itself down. A zero-value sink or an output gate fixes that; StreamingLLM, Qwen's gating, and when the two are equivalent.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 6 个章节
#Attention#Gated Attention#StreamingLLM#KV Cache#paper reading

Give every value in one attention head the scalar 1. However the queries and keys change, and wherever softmax puts its probability, the weighted sum is always exactly 1. The head can decide where its content comes from, but it has no way to shrink its own output: to make it 0.1, or to write nothing at all.

Add one position whose value is 0, and the problem is solved. Put 90% of the probability on it and the output becomes 0.1. On an attention heatmap that position hogs most of the weight, yet what it writes into the result is 0; what it really changes is everyone else’s contribution.

This construction only shows what kind of computation a sink can perform; it doesn’t prove every model needs one. The same constraint shows up in two practical places, each with its own response:

  • On the cache side: once the KV cache has a fixed length, the earliest positions get evicted. If an evicted position was a sink, the remaining content gets scaled up all at once. StreamingLLM therefore keeps the first two positions permanently.
  • On the architecture side: Qwen’s Gated Attention inserts a coefficient computed from the current input after SDPA and before the output projection, directly shrinking the whole head’s output.

The two act in different places. Removing a sink reallocates shares inside softmax’s denominator, so others get more probability and the remaining content is amplified. Output gating comes later: it multiplies the finished vector by a coefficient below 1, leaving the proportions between content unchanged.

We’ll start with the convex-combination constraint, then look at what each mechanism does in its source code, and finish by deriving exactly when a zero-value sink and an output gate are equivalent, and when they aren’t. All numerical experiments ran on CPU, with no model weights downloaded and no training.

Fundamentals: scores, probabilities and outputs are different things

Let the current position’s input hidden state be xix_ixi​, projected to a query qiq_iqi​; historical positions provide keys kjk_jkj​ and values vjv_jvj​. Leaving out positional encoding and mask details, one head computes

sij=qikj⊤dh,pij=esij∑kesik,ui=∑jpijvj.s_{ij}=\frac{q_i k_j^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}, \qquad p_{ij}=\frac{e^{s_{ij}}}{\sum_k e^{s_{ik}}}, \qquad u_i=\sum_jp_{ij}v_j.sij​=dh​​qi​kj⊤​​,pij​=∑k​esik​esij​​,ui​=j∑​pij​vj​.

dhd_hdh​ is the head width. Multiple heads’ uiu_iui​ are concatenated, passed through the output projection WOW_OWO​ and written into the residual branch. That’s three distinct objects: scores sss that express preference, normalized probabilities ppp, and the vector uuu that actually enters later computation. Heatmaps usually show only the middle one.

Since pij≥0p_{ij}\ge0pij​≥0 and they sum to 1, uiu_iui​ always lies in the convex hull of the vjv_jvj​: the region you can reach by mixing those vectors with non-negative weights summing to 1. If every value is 1 in some dimension, the output in that dimension can only be 1. Add a zero vector, and the origin joins the hull, giving the output room to shrink.

Real models have other ways out: values can cancel each other, WOW_OWO​ can change direction and scale, and the residual branch can adjust together with other sublayers.

StreamingLLM observed that several trained models repeatedly assign large attention to initial positions, and connected this to probability normalization and to the initial positions being visible to every later query. When Attention Sink Emerges further examined training and architecture conditions; in its setting, a sink acts more like a key bias that absorbs excess probability, and the normalization scheme affects how it forms.

Being always visible makes initial positions suited to this role; whether a sink actually forms still depends on the learned keys, values and training. You can’t diagnose a sink from a large key norm, and you can’t infer semantic importance from high attention weight. To see downstream influence, you have to multiply in the value.

Dropping a sink changes the whole row’s normalization

Fix one query. Call a candidate sink’s probability aaa and its value vsv_svs​, and let μ\muμ be the output of the other positions renormalized among themselves. The full result is

u=avs+(1−a)μ.u=a v_s+(1-a)\mu.u=avs​+(1−a)μ.

Drop just that position, leaving every other logit and value unchanged, and the result becomes μ\muμ, so

udrop−u=a(μ−vs).u_{\mathrm{drop}}-u=a(\mu-v_s).udrop​−u=a(μ−vs​).

The opening example with vs=0v_s=0vs​=0 is the special case. When μ≠0\mu\ne0μ=0, the magnitude after dropping is 1/(1−a)1/(1-a)1/(1−a) times the original: a=0.9a=0.9a=0.9 gives 10×, a=0.99a=0.99a=0.99 gives 100×.

As a check on the general formula, three random seeds, four value dimensions and eight probabilities gave 96 FP64 computations with max absolute residual 8.88×10−168.88\times10^{-16}8.88×10−16.

The dropped position’s direct content contribution can be tiny while its share of the denominator is huge, and after it’s dropped, all the remaining content is rescaled up together. This identity analyzes one row of one attention call; inside a full Transformer the downstream inputs change too, so these row differences can’t simply be added up into final model error.

StreamingLLM: keep two pieces of the cache

Autoregressive generation keeps accumulating historical KV. The most direct fixed-length policy keeps only a recent window and evicts the oldest entries once it’s full. If the initial positions happen to be acting as sinks, the normalization change from the previous section happens the first time they slide out of the window.

StreamingLLM keeps two pieces of cache: a few initial KVs plus a recent stretch. It doesn’t compress the whole history, and it doesn’t estimate a retention value for each old token; the initial positions are always kept, and everything in the middle is dropped.

The core of the authors’ StartRecentKVCache key selection is just this:

torch.cat(
    [
        self.k_slice(k, 0, self.start_size),
        self.k_slice(k, seq_len - self.recent_size, seq_len),
    ],
    dim=self.k_seq_dim,
)

Values are sliced the same way. If the sequence hasn’t exceeded capacity, the cache is returned as-is; only then are the two pieces concatenated. With start_size=2, recent_size=14, 16 positions are kept, two of them permanently given to the start. Compared with a pure sliding window, that’s two fewer recent tokens.

Feeding tensors numbered 0–63 into this real cache class in turn, under both 0+16 and 2+14, gives 128 CPU calls whose kept indices match the rule. Using those indices to index two constructed attention inputs then shows both the benefit and the cost of the same rule.

Both results hold at once. Keeping initial KVs makes already-learned normalization behavior more continuous, and the price is less room for recent content. StreamingLLM’s streaming ability doesn’t mean the model can still retrieve from the full dropped history.

Cache positions have to match RoPE

A real implementation can’t stop at those two slices. RoPE encodes position as a rotation of queries and keys, so the dot product depends on relative position. After dropping a stretch of middle tokens, getting the kept KVs to use contiguous cache positions requires knowing exactly where the rotation is applied.

In the authors’ Llama position-shift implementation, the cache receives the unrotated key, and only the key used for this attention call is rotated by contiguous positions:

past_key_value = (key_states, value_states) if use_cache else None

key_position_ids = torch.arange(kv_seq_len, device=position_ids.device).unsqueeze(0)
key_states = apply_rotary_pos_emb_single(key_states, cos, sin, key_position_ids)

For example, if the kept history tokens are [0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8] and the next query is token 9, the computation uses positions organized by the cache. Dropping the middle gap changes the relative distance between the start and the recent content. It isn’t a uniform shift of every position, and it isn’t just moving memory around.

If the cache stored keys already rotated by their old positions, rotating again by new positions would stack rotations. Caching unrotated keys and letting each computation generate rotations for current positions is the key ordering in this code. The CPU experiments above ran only the trimming class and constructed attention, not the full Llama RoPE shift, and measured no perplexity.

Gated attention: scaling after the weighted sum

There’s a more direct approach from the architecture side. One of the gating positions studied in Gated Attention sits right after the SDPA output and before the output projection.

Let the current query’s pre-norm hidden state be xix_ixi​, with the head output still ui=∑jpijvju_i=\sum_jp_{ij}v_jui​=∑j​pij​vj​. A gate is generated from the same xix_ixi​:

gi=σ(xiWG),u~i=ui⊙gi.g_i=\sigma(x_iW_G),\qquad \widetilde u_i=u_i\odot g_i.gi​=σ(xi​WG​),ui​=ui​⊙gi​.

A headwise gate gives the whole head one coefficient; an elementwise gate generates one per channel within the head. Both take effect only after this call’s softmax is done, so neither reallocates this call’s pijp_{ij}pij​. Heads are concatenated and passed through WOW_OWO​ afterwards.

The authors’ pinned Qwen3Attention source folds the gate projection into the same q_proj as the query projection. In the elementwise version, the output is twice the width of an ordinary query, then split into query and gate by KV group; the gate’s final shape is [batch, length, heads, head_dim].

That just merges one linear projection; it doesn’t mean the gate takes part in the QK dot product. The split-off query builds attention probabilities as usual, and the gate branch waits until here:

attn_output = attn_output.transpose(1, 2).contiguous()

if self.headwise_attn_output_gate or self.elementwise_attn_output_gate:
    attn_output = attn_output * torch.sigmoid(gate_score)

attn_output = attn_output.reshape(bsz, q_len, -1)
attn_output = self.o_proj(attn_output)

Where this sits in the code determines both the broadcast and what the channels mean. Moving a per-channel gate after WOW_OWO​ generally won’t give the same result: the output projection has already mixed channels, and a matrix multiply doesn’t commute with an arbitrary diagonal scaling.

To observe just this one thing, I extracted the eager forward function from the pinned source and ran it with small CPU projection layers. With Q/K/V held identical and headwise and elementwise gates added separately, over sequence lengths 1, 7 and 16 and seeds 0, 17 and 42, that’s 18 comparisons. The returned attention probabilities differ by exactly zero, and outputs match an independent reconstruction of “original SDPA output times the corresponding gate” exactly.

This is a forward-mechanism check. What the paper reports is that sinks weaken in gated models after training, which involves Q/K and intermediate activations changing during training. §4 of the paper connects that with query-dependent, head-specific output sparsity. Multiplying a gate into a fixed-weight model doesn’t reproduce that training result, and in this eager implementation it doesn’t even save the QK or PV computation, because SDPA is already done when the multiply happens.

When is a zero-value sink the same as a gate?

The opening output of 0.1 can be produced by a zero-value sink holding 90% of the probability, or by multiplying the ordinary output by 0.1. For one fixed query, the two really can match exactly.

Let the ordinary positions’ exponent sum be Z=∑jesjZ=\sum_j e^{s_j}Z=∑j​esj​ and their self-normalized output μ\muμ. Add a sink with logit ccc and value zero:

usink=ZZ+ecμ.u_{\mathrm{sink}}=\frac Z{Z+e^c}\mu.usink​=Z+ecZ​μ.

For this to equal a scalar gate’s gμg\mugμ with 0<g<10<g<10<g<1, just take

c=log⁡Z+log⁡1−gg.c=\log Z+\log\frac{1-g}{g}.c=logZ+logg1−g​.

g=0g=0g=0 and g=1g=1g=1 correspond to infinite-logit limits. The key detail is that ZZZ appears in the matching value: change the ordinary keys and query, and the required sink score changes too.

Where they diverge: a common logit offset

Add the same offset bbb to every ordinary logit. The softmax distribution among ordinary tokens doesn’t change, and neither does μ\muμ, but ZZZ gets multiplied by ebe^beb. So a sink with a fixed logit changes its scaling, while a fixed output gate doesn’t.

The fixed c=0c=0c=0 sink’s output moves from near zero toward 1, while the fixed g=0.2g=0.2g=0.2 output stays at 0.2. The sink matched per input by the formula above differs from the gate by at most 1.94×10−161.94\times10^{-16}1.94×10−16.

A real sink’s score does vary with the query, so it isn’t necessarily constant. But whether a fixed key’s dot product with the query can realize log⁡Z+log⁡((1−g)/g)\log Z+\log((1-g)/g)logZ+log((1−g)/g) for every input still needs proving, since ZZZ aggregates the exponent scores of all ordinary keys. Hand-computing a ccc for each input doesn’t establish a general equivalence between the two parameterizations.

Where they diverge: per-channel scaling

An elementwise gate changes another condition. A zero-value sink can only scale all channels uniformly. If the ordinary output is

μ=(1,1.118615),\mu=(1,1.118615),μ=(1,1.118615),

any scalar gate lands on the line gμg\mugμ. Per-channel coefficients (0.2,0.8)(0.2,0.8)(0.2,0.8), however, give

μ~=(0.2,0.894892).\widetilde\mu=(0.2,0.894892).μ​=(0.2,0.894892).

The direction has changed: the best-fit scalar is about 0.533487, and the remaining Euclidean distance is still about 0.447317.

If all logits and values were allowed to retrain, the reachable set would change, so the distance in the figure isn’t an expressiveness proof for every attention parameterization.

What gating adds to the value path can be written out through the projections. With current attention weights fixed,

(∑jpijxjWV)WO=∑jpijxj(WVWO).\left(\sum_jp_{ij}x_jW_V\right)W_O =\sum_jp_{ij}x_j(W_VW_O).(j∑​pij​xj​WV​)WO​=j∑​pij​xj​(WV​WO​).

Ordinary value and output projections can merge into one matrix. With a per-channel gate introducing a query-dependent diagonal matrix DiD_iDi​, the merged term becomes

WVDiWO.W_VD_iW_O.WV​Di​WO​.

So different queries can make different choices among the same value channels. What’s added is an input-dependent transform. Full attention already depends nonlinearly on input through Q/K and softmax, so gating isn’t “the first nonlinearity.” And for a fixed query, this product’s rank still can’t exceed the head width; adding a diagonal matrix doesn’t break that bound.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows, exactly, that dropping a sink with probability aaa rescales the remaining content by 1/(1−a)1/(1-a)1/(1−a), and that StreamingLLM’s real cache class keeps exactly the start+recent positions it claims.
  • It shows, on constructed inputs, that keeping start positions preserves normalization while losing retrieval of a middle token two steps earlier than a pure window.
  • It shows, by running the pinned Qwen source, that output gating leaves this call’s attention probabilities untouched and multiplies after SDPA.
  • It shows a zero-value sink and a scalar gate match for one query only with an input-dependent logit, and that a per-channel gate reaches outputs no scalar sink can.
  • It doesn’t download model weights, train anything, run the full Llama RoPE shift, measure perplexity or reproduce the paper’s finding that gating weakens sinks after training.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. Softmax makes a head’s output a convex combination of its values, so a head that wants to write less needs either a zero-ish value to absorb probability (a sink) or a way to scale after the fact (a gate). The three operations in this post use different inputs:

  • A sink changes how much share other content gets inside probability normalization, looking only at this forward pass’s scores.
  • An output gate adjusts heads or channels after content is aggregated, also looking only at current weights.
  • StreamingLLM decides which positions stay in the cache.

The two schemes can coexist. But because both ease some kind of sink phenomenon doesn’t mean two cache positions can simply be replaced by one multiplication.

A sink and a gate can produce the same number for one query; they’re different mechanisms the moment the inputs change.

Environment and measurement method

Author-function checks use CPU PyTorch 2.14.1; curves use Python 3.13.5, NumPy 2.4.4 and Matplotlib 3.11.0. No model weights were downloaded, no GPU benchmarks run and no language model trained. StreamingLLM is MIT licensed; Gated Attention’s root is MIT, the excerpted file keeps its Apache-2.0 header, and the snapshot keeps both attributions.

In the gating comparison, RoPE is the identity rotation, dropout is zero, the causal mask is kept and WOW_OWO​ is the identity so head output can be read directly. Projections and multiplies use FP64, with softmax converted to FP32 and back as in the authors’ implementation. That’s nine base forward passes plus eighteen gated ones, not full Qwen model inference.

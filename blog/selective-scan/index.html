Why a Serial Loop Beat My Parallel Selective Scans

A plain time-serial kernel was fastest in 11 of 12 shapes on an RTX 5070 Laptop, up to 24× ahead of the parallel scans. Only the narrowest state, N=32, let a chunked scan win.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 11 个章节
#CUDA#Selective Scan#SSM#Mamba#parallel scan#atomic#performance measurement

I timed six hand-written CUDA kernels for Mamba’s selective scan on an RTX 5070 Laptop GPU. Four of them break the scan’s dependency on time with chunked parallel scans; two just walk the sequence one step at a time. The serial walk won 11 of the 12 shapes I measured. At 8,192 time steps, ssm.cu took 3.0 ms, and the chunked scans took 6.0 to 13.0 ms.

That isn’t because the parallel kernels are badly written. They do extra work to remove a dependency, and at these sizes the dependency wasn’t what limited the GPU: there were already enough independent sequences to keep it busy. The one shape where a chunked scan won, by about 1.25×, is the one with the narrowest state.

Here’s the plan. I’ll set up the operator and the one algebraic fact every parallel version relies on, with a two-step example you can check by hand. Then I’ll walk through the three designs, look at what Mamba’s own kernel does differently, and go through the measurements, including a “bug” I first thought one of the files had, and why it turned out not to be one.

Fundamentals: a recurrence with a data-dependent decay

The scan takes five inputs and produces one output. For a batch of BBB sequences, sequence length LLL, model width DDD and state width NNN:

u, Δ, y∈RB×L×D,A∈RD×N,B,C∈RB×L×N,skip∈RD.u,\ \Delta,\ y\in\mathbb R^{B\times L\times D},\qquad A\in\mathbb R^{D\times N},\qquad B,C\in\mathbb R^{B\times L\times N},\qquad \text{skip}\in\mathbb R^{D}.u, Δ, y∈RB×L×D,A∈RD×N,B,C∈RB×L×N,skip∈RD.

For every (b, t, d, n) the two coefficient arrays are

at,d,n=exp⁡(Δt,d Ad,n),bt,d,n=Δt,d ut,d Bt,n,a_{t,d,n}=\exp(\Delta_{t,d}\,A_{d,n}),\qquad b_{t,d,n}=\Delta_{t,d}\,u_{t,d}\,B_{t,n},at,d,n​=exp(Δt,d​Ad,n​),bt,d,n​=Δt,d​ut,d​Bt,n​,

and the state and output are

ht,d,n=at,d,n ht−1,d,n+bt,d,n,h−1,d,n=0,h_{t,d,n}=a_{t,d,n}\,h_{t-1,d,n}+b_{t,d,n},\qquad h_{-1,d,n}=0,ht,d,n​=at,d,n​ht−1,d,n​+bt,d,n​,h−1,d,n​=0, yt,d=∑n=0N−1Ct,n ht,d,n+skipd ut,d.y_{t,d}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}C_{t,n}\,h_{t,d,n}+\text{skip}_d\,u_{t,d}.yt,d​=n=0∑N−1​Ct,n​ht,d,n​+skipd​ut,d​.

That’s the whole operator. There’s no input projection, causal convolution, softplus on Δ\DeltaΔ, gate or backward pass in these files: they implement the inner scan of a Mamba block, not the block.

Each output needs the state from the previous step, and that state is scaled by a decay that depends on the current input. You can’t get hth_tht​ without first having ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​. Every design below is an argument about how much of that walk can be avoided.

The one fact parallel scans rely on

One step is an affine map, h↦a h+bh\mapsto a\,h+bh↦ah+b, and affine maps compose into affine maps. A chunk of steps is therefore one map h↦α h+βh\mapsto \alpha\,h+\betah↦αh+β, and two chunks, an earlier “left” one and a later “right” one, combine as

α=αright αleft,β=αright βleft+βright.\alpha=\alpha_{\text{right}}\,\alpha_{\text{left}},\qquad \beta=\alpha_{\text{right}}\,\beta_{\text{left}}+\beta_{\text{right}}.α=αright​αleft​,β=αright​βleft​+βright​.

Check it by hand with two identical steps, a=0.5a=0.5a=0.5 and b=1b=1b=1, starting from h=0h=0h=0. Step by step: h=0.5⋅0+1=1h=0.5\cdot0+1=1h=0.5⋅0+1=1, then h=0.5⋅1+1=1.5h=0.5\cdot1+1=1.5h=0.5⋅1+1=1.5. Combined: α=0.5⋅0.5=0.25\alpha=0.5\cdot0.5=0.25α=0.5⋅0.5=0.25 and β=0.5⋅1+1=1.5\beta=0.5\cdot1+1=1.5β=0.5⋅1+1=1.5, so the chunk maps 000 to 0.25⋅0+1.5=1.50.25\cdot0+1.5=1.50.25⋅0+1.5=1.5. Same answer.

Because the combination is associative, you can compute every chunk’s (α,β)(\alpha,\beta)(α,β) in parallel and then combine them with a parallel prefix scan. There’s one more consequence that matters later: the scan always starts from h−1=0h_{-1}=0h−1​=0, so the state at the start of any chunk is just the β\betaβ of everything before it. The α\alphaα of the earlier chunks multiplies zero.

Three designs for one recurrence

The six files are three answers to the same question, two files each:

  1. Keep time serial, parallelize the state. One warp or block owns a (batch, d_model) pair, keeps the NNN states on chip and loops over time: ssm.cu and selective_scan_rewrite.cu.
  2. Chunked scan with atomics. Compute each chunk’s (α,β)(\alpha,\beta)(α,β), scan the chunk summaries into prefixes, replay each chunk from its prefix and atomicAdd each state’s contribution into yyy: ssm_seq.cu and selective_scan_improved.cu.
  3. Chunked scan with a deterministic reduction. The same decomposition, but contributions go to a buffer that a second kernel sums in a fixed order, so the output doesn’t depend on which atomic lands first: selective_scan_seq_parallel.cu and selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu.

Figure 2: The three designs. The first keeps time inside one block; the other two expose per-chunk summaries and combine them across blocks.

What “parallel” has to buy

The time-serial design has B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D independent chains. Each chain is LLL steps long, and each step updates NNN states. In the shapes I measured, B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D is between 64 and 512, so even before any scan trick there are 64 to 512 chains for the GPU’s 36 SMs. What a chain lacks is work within a step: NNN multiply-adds and one reduction over NNN, which must finish before the next step starts.

A parallel scan replaces that dependency with more work: chunk-local scans, a prefix scan, a replay, and either atomics or a reduction buffer. The question is whether the extra work costs less than the dependency it removes.

Design 1: one chain per warp or block

ssm.cu has four kernels: a warp or a block per chain, with the state in shared memory or, if it doesn’t fit, in a device buffer. solve uses the warp kernel when N≤64N\le64N≤64 and the block kernel above that. For most shapes here that means the warp kernel with shared state. Its time loop:

for (int t = 0; t < seq_len; ++t) {
  // load u, delta, B, C for this (b, t, d)
  float local_sum = 0.f;
  for (int i = lane; i < d_state / 4; i += 32) {   // one float4 of states per lane
    float4 hv = h4[i];
    local_sum += update_state4(hv, a4[i], b4[i], c4[i], delta_val, delta_u);
    h4[i] = hv;
  }
  const float sum = warp_reduce_sum(local_sum);
  if (lane == 0) y[udy] = sum + skip_d * u_val;
}

update_state4 is the arithmetic core: four states per float4, one expf and one FMA each, plus their share of C⋅hC\cdot hC⋅h. Each lane handles one float4, so at N=64N=64N=64 sixteen of the 32 lanes work and at N=32N=32N=32 only eight do. The state never leaves the chip, and the only global write per step is one yyy.

The compiled kernel is small: 958 instructions, with 67 FFMA, 12 MUFU.EX2 for the expfs, and no atomics or barriers, since a warp needs no barrier. With B=1B=1B=1 and D=64D=64D=64 there are 64 warps spread over 36 SMs, one or two per SM, far below the 48 an SM can hold. So the kernel waits on the loop-carried dependency rather than on throughput.

selective_scan_rewrite.cu is the same idea with one block per chain, register-resident state and a block-wide reduction. That reduction costs shuffles across all the block’s warps plus barriers on every step: 45 SHFL and 6 BAR.SYNC in its SASS.

Design 2: chunked scan with atomics

ssm_seq.cu and selective_scan_improved.cu share one design with two paths.

Short sequences (L≤1024L\le1024L≤1024). One block per (b, d, n) covers the whole sequence: the block is next_pow2(L) threads, a Hillis–Steele scan in shared memory combines the steps’ (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) pairs, and every thread adds C · h for its step into yyy with atomicAdd. A separate kernel first writes skip · u into yyy. At L=1024L=1024L=1024 and the default shape that’s 4,096 blocks of 1,024 threads.

Long sequences. The sequence splits into chunks of 256 steps, and four kernels run in turn:

  1. write skip · u into yyy;
  2. scan each chunk, store every step’s local (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) pair, and write the chunk’s summary (α,β)(\alpha, \beta)(α,β);
  3. scan the chunk summaries of each state into exclusive prefixes;
  4. replay each step from its chunk’s prefix, h = local_a · prefix + local_b, and atomicAdd C · h into yyy.

That’s one atomic per (state, time step). At L=8192L=8192L=8192 with D=N=64D=N=64D=N=64 that’s 4,096 states × 8,192 steps, or about 33.5 million atomics: 64 into each output element. The stored local pairs take 2×4096×8192×42\times4096\times8192\times42×4096×8192×4 bytes, or 256 MiB, written by step 2 and read back by step 4, for an operator whose inputs total 8 MiB.

The bug that wasn’t

ssm_seq.cu keeps only the β\betaβ half of each prefix. selective_scan_improved.cu keeps both halves, and a comment there says keeping α\alphaα “fixes the old code’s loss of affine scale information”. I first read that as a correctness bug in ssm_seq.cu that the test data happened to hide.

It isn’t one. As Figure 1 shows, the scan starts from h−1=0h_{-1}=0h−1​=0, so the prefix’s α\alphaα only ever multiplies zero. Both files replay with exactly the same expression, local_a · prefix_b + local_b, and selective_scan_improved.cu reads its stored prefix_a only to discard it ((void)prefix_a;). The code comment goes on to say why it’s kept: so the kernel could later accept a non-zero initial state. That’s a sensible interface choice, not a fix. The two files compute the same operator, and their errors agree.

What does differ is memory management. On every call, ssm_seq.cu allocates its four scratch buffers with cudaMalloc and releases them with cudaFree, which waits for the whole device. selective_scan_improved.cu sizes one workspace and allocates it with stream-ordered cudaMallocAsync/cudaFreeAsync, which reuse memory from the driver’s pool. It can also take a caller-owned workspace.

For sequences longer than 1,024 chunks (262,144 steps), selective_scan_improved.cu falls back to a kernel that recomputes every output from time zero. That’s O(L2)O(L^2)O(L2) work, kept only as a correctness escape hatch.

Design 3: chunked scan with a deterministic reduction

selective_scan_seq_parallel.cu runs the same chunk scan and prefix scan. Instead of adding into yyy, it writes each state’s contribution into a buffer, contrib[state, t], and a final kernel sums the NNN contributions of each output in a fixed order. Its workspace is 12 bytes per state-step: local aaa, local bbb and the contribution. At L=8192L=8192L=8192 that’s 384 MiB.

It has a static limit worth knowing about. MAX_CHUNKS allows 4,096 chunks, but the prefix kernel uses one thread per chunk and caps its block at 1,024 threads. A sequence longer than 1024×256=262,1441024\times256=262{,}1441024×256=262,144 steps would get prefixes for only its first 1,024 chunks. Nothing here comes close, but it would surface with very long contexts.

selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu avoids storing per-step pairs at all:

  1. scan each chunk and keep only its summary;
  2. scan the summaries in two levels, within groups of chunks and then across groups;
  3. for a tile of 32 states at a time, re-run each chunk from its prefix and write a partial output per tile;
  4. sum the tiles and add skip · u.

Its only large buffer is the partial output, one copy per state tile, or twice the output size at N=64N=64N=64. It’s the most memory-frugal of the four parallel designs.

What Mamba’s own kernel does

The reference kernel in mamba_ssm runs the whole scan in one launch and never writes chunk summaries to global memory.

flowchart TD
    G["one thread block per (batch, channel)"] --> CH["for each chunk of time steps, in order"]
    CH --> ST["for each state n"]
    ST --> SC["block-wide scan over the chunk's steps: each thread holds consecutive steps, CUB combines their (a, b) pairs"]
    SC --> PF["keep the chunk's last state as the next chunk's starting prefix"]
    SC --> ACC["add C·h into this chunk's outputs, held in registers"]
    ACC --> W["after the state loop: write this chunk's y"]
    PF -. "next chunk" .-> CH

Figure 3: Mamba’s fused forward kernel. It is parallel over time within a chunk, serial over chunks and states, and carries the prefix in shared memory.

So Mamba does parallelize over time. It uses the same affine scan as design 2, but within one block, with the chunks processed in order and the running prefix carried along. Nothing crosses blocks, so there are no atomics, no scratch arrays and no extra launches.

I didn’t build or time Mamba’s kernel for this post. It’s the obvious next comparison, and nothing below says how it would rank.

How I measured

All six files expose the same solve(...) entry point, so one harness times them all. It allocates the inputs and output on the device, calls solve once to warm up and five more times, then times 30 calls between two CUDA events. Each number is therefore the time per solve call, including whatever the file does inside that call: allocation, frees and every launch. It is not pure kernel time. Each cell is the median of three such runs.

Inputs are uniform in [−0.5,0.5][-0.5, 0.5][−0.5,0.5], except Δ∈[0.01,0.06]\Delta\in[0.01,0.06]Δ∈[0.01,0.06] and A∈[−0.6,−0.1]A\in[-0.6,-0.1]A∈[−0.6,−0.1], so each step’s decay a=exp⁡(ΔA)a=\exp(\Delta A)a=exp(ΔA) lies in [0.965,0.999][0.965, 0.999][0.965,0.999]. After timing, the harness compares yyy with a CPU double-precision recurrence on the same inputs.

The GPU is an RTX 5070 Laptop (8 GiB, SM 120). Its clocks move: the first run of a shape could take twice as long as the next (0.73 ms vs. 0.38 ms for the same ssm.cu call), which is why I report medians and only trust large differences.

The time-serial kernel wins, and scales the way a serial loop should

ssm.cu is fastest in 11 of 12 rows. The other time-serial file, selective_scan_rewrite.cu, is closest: 1.22–1.24× slower across the sequence-length rows and between 1.06× (B=4B=4B=4) and 1.45× (D=256D=256D=256) on the others. That’s the price of its block-wide reduction on every step. The four parallel files are further behind, from 1.33× to 23.8×: the largest gap is selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu at B=8B=8B=8, 5.145 ms against 0.216 ms.

The scaling of ssm.cu is what a latency-bound loop predicts:

  • Linear in LLL: 0.098 ms at 256 steps, 3.010 ms at 8,192, 30.7× the time for 32× the steps. More steps are more iterations, and nothing hides them.
  • Nearly flat in DDD and BBB: 0.382, 0.386 and 0.391 ms for D=64D=64D=64, 128 and 256. B=8B=8B=8 at L=512L=512L=512 does 8× the work of B=1B=1B=1 in 1.12× the time, and B=4B=4B=4 at L=1024L=1024L=1024 does 4× in 1.19×. More chains mean more warps running side by side until the SMs fill up.
  • Nearly flat in NNN: 0.382, 0.382 and 0.389 ms for N=32N=32N=32, 64 and 128, even though N=128N=128N=128 switches to the block kernel. The extra arithmetic per step hides under the loop’s latency.

That last point is exactly what the parallel designs try to buy: they spend more total work to remove the serial dependency. At these sizes the dependency was already overlapped across chains.

The chunked scans pay a cost they don’t recover

ssm_seq.cu stays within 1.55× of ssm.cu while it uses its short path, then switches to the chunked path above 1,024 steps and jumps: 0.590 ms at 1,024, 4.195 ms at 2,048 (×7.1 for twice the steps), 13.015 ms at 8,192. At 8,192 steps that’s four kernels, 256 MiB of stored pairs written and read back, and 33.5 million atomics.

selective_scan_improved.cu runs the same kernels and is 2.2× faster on long sequences: 6.010 ms against 13.015 ms at 8,192 steps. With B=1B=1B=1, the two files take the same time, within 1%, on every shape that stays on the short path, where neither allocates. They only diverge once the chunked path’s scratch buffers appear. That pattern fits the allocation difference: cudaMalloc and a device-synchronizing cudaFree on every call, against stream-ordered allocation from a pool. I haven’t isolated it, though. A run with a caller-owned workspace for both files would settle it. Separately, at B=4B=4B=4 and B=8B=8B=8 the two files differ even on the short path (3.284 vs. 2.602 ms and 3.178 vs. 2.353 ms), which allocation doesn’t explain and which I haven’t traced.

Both atomic files grow faster than DDD on the short path: 0.590, 1.247 and 2.860 ms for D=64D=64D=64, 128 and 256. Their block count is B⋅D⋅NB\cdot D\cdot NB⋅D⋅N, from 4,096 blocks of 1,024 threads to 16,384, while ssm.cu simply gets more chains to overlap.

The two deterministic files avoid atomics and pay in memory traffic instead. Both are slower than selective_scan_improved.cu on long sequences: 8.403 and 6.803 ms at 8,192 steps against 6.010. selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu is the slowest file on short sequences (0.482 ms at 256 steps, five times ssm.cu), where its extra launches dominate. On long ones it overtakes selective_scan_seq_parallel.cu, since it never stores per-step pairs.

The one row where a scan wins

At N=32N=32N=32 the order flips: selective_scan_improved.cu (0.303 ms) and ssm_seq.cu (0.305 ms) beat ssm.cu (0.382 ms) by about 1.25×.

The reason is in the scaling above. Halving NNN halves the short path’s work, from 4,096 blocks to 2,048, and its time drops with it (0.590 → 0.305 ms). ssm.cu doesn’t get faster with a narrower state, because its cost is the loop’s latency, not the arithmetic. Fewer states also means fewer atomics per output, 32 instead of 64. So a chunked scan can win, but only when there is little work per step for the serial kernel to hide behind.

The same reasoning predicts the scans should win when B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D is small, with too few chains to fill the GPU. I didn’t measure that. It’s cheap to test, since smaller B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D shrinks the scratch buffers, and it’s the experiment I’d run next.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows that on this GPU, for these 12 shapes, a time-serial kernel beats chunked parallel scans in 11 of 12 cases, by 1.06× to 23.8×, with all six files agreeing to within 3.1×10−73.1\times10^{-7}3.1×10−7.
  • It shows why: the serial kernel’s cost is flat in BBB, DDD and NNN up to these sizes, while the parallel scans add launches, scratch traffic and atomics that grow with the problem.
  • It shows one exception, N=32N=32N=32, where a chunked scan wins by about 1.25×.
  • It doesn’t show where the crossover sits for small B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D, very long sequences, or the larger batches and widths of real training. None of those were measured.
  • It doesn’t explain every gap. The 2.2× between the two atomic files is attributed to allocation by elimination, not by an isolating experiment, and their difference at B=4B=4B=4 and B=8B=8B=8 is unexplained.
  • It doesn’t compare against Mamba’s fused kernel, which wasn’t built or timed.
  • The numbers are per-call times on a laptop whose clocks vary. Differences of 10–20% between two files aren’t a reliable ranking; the 2× and larger gaps held across runs. Nsight Compute was unavailable (see the appendix), so there are no stall reasons or occupancy figures, only event timings and static SASS counts.

Conclusion

Six kernels, three designs, one operator. Keeping time serial and parallelizing only the state looks naive, but on this GPU and these shapes it was the fastest design almost everywhere. That’s because many independent sequences already hide the loop’s latency. The chunked scans remove the dependency at the cost of extra launches, scratch memory and atomics, and that cost only paid back when the state was narrow. Mamba’s own kernel suggests the way out: keep the chunked scan, but inside one block, so none of that cost crosses the grid.

A parallel scan has to remove a bottleneck you actually have; with enough independent sequences, the serial loop was never the bottleneck.

Appendix

Environment and measurement method

  • GPU: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Laptop, 8151 MiB, SM 120, driver 610.88.

  • CUDA 13.1 (V13.1.115) with the Visual Studio 2026 toolset (MSVC 14.51), which CUDA 13.1 doesn’t officially support. -allow-unsupported-compiler alone made cudafe++ crash with an access violation; adding -D_ALLOW_COMPILER_AND_STL_VERSION_MISMATCH fixed it, because the crash is an STL version mismatch between CUDA’s headers and MSVC’s:

    nvcc -std=c++17 -O3 -lineinfo -arch=sm_120 \
     -allow-unsupported-compiler \
     -D_ALLOW_COMPILER_AND_STL_VERSION_MISMATCH \
     -c selective_scan.cu -o selective_scan.obj

  • Timing: cudaEvent around 30 solve calls after 6 warm-up calls; median of 3 runs per cell.

  • Reference: CPU double-precision recurrence on the same inputs; maximum absolute error.

  • Profiling: ncu 2026.2.1 was installed, but the driver refused performance counters (ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM), which needs an administrator to change. SASS instruction counts come from cuobjdump -sass; they are static counts, not measurements of stalls.

Source

All six kernels are in Murmurwkk/cuda-operators, selective_scan/:

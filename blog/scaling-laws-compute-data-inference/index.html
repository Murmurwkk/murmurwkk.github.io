Why the Scaling-Law Training Recipe Keeps Changing

Training 7B on 140B tokens costs about what 70B on 14B does. Kaplan vs. Chinchilla, repeated data, serving cost and inference-time compute each change which one you should train.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 8 个章节
#Scaling Laws#Chinchilla#training budget#data efficiency#paper deep dive

Training a 7B model on 140B tokens costs roughly the same compute as training a 70B model on 14B tokens.

The difference is elsewhere. A bigger model can hold more; a smaller one can read more data for the same budget. And this isn’t free trial and error: once both big models are trained, the budget is gone. The value of scaling laws is that, after a few small experiments, you can make a prediction about this choice before spending it.

But one curve of “how much better per order of magnitude of parameters” isn’t enough. The two configurations above change parameters and training data at the same time. If the model will be deployed heavily, weight size matters. If the corpus has run out, reading it again isn’t the same as getting a new one. The recipes in the literature keep changing, and most of the reason hides in those conditions.

This post tracks the recipe through five changes of condition. First we’ll make “better” measurable and look at why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed, then solve for the optimum under a fixed budget. After that we add, one at a time, finite data, coupling between parameters and data, deployment cost, and inference-time compute. Each one moves the answer, and we’ll see why.

Making “better” a number

Language model training is usually judged by validation cross-entropy: the higher the probability assigned to the correct next token, the smaller the negative log-probability, averaged over many tokens. It’s smoother than “must get the whole question right” accuracy and shows scaling regularities more easily. The price is that average prediction loss and accuracy on a given task aren’t the same objective.

Let NNN be dense model parameters and DDD the total tokens processed in training. When compute is dominated by parameter matmuls, training FLOPs are commonly estimated as

C≈6ND.C\approx6ND.C≈6ND.

Forward is about 2N2N2N FLOPs per token, backward about twice forward, giving the 6. Long-context attention, activation recomputation, communication and hardware utilization aren’t included. So 6ND6ND6ND compares the arithmetic budget of a model family; it doesn’t convert to GPU hours, and MoE or very long contexts can’t just plug in total parameters.

Act 1: Kaplan vs. Chinchilla

Kaplan et al. found loss falls approximately as a power law over a wide range: scale keeps helping, with diminishing returns. Their compute-allocation analysis favored big models, giving N∗∝C0.73N^*\propto C^{0.73}N∗∝C0.73. The experiments mainly used WebText2, counted parameters excluding embeddings, mostly used context 1024, and reached about 1.5B non-embedding parameters (paper §2, §3, §6).

Chinchilla re-swept model size and training length. Besides tracing the lower envelope of loss along training curves, it directly compared configurations at fixed FLOPs: the IsoFLOP experiments. Over 400 models from under 70M to over 16B parameters, trained on 5B to over 400B tokens, three estimation methods gave parameter/data growth exponents of about 0.50/0.500.50/0.500.50/0.50, 0.49/0.510.49/0.510.49/0.51 and 0.46/0.540.46/0.540.46/0.54 (paper §3, Table 2).

The learning-rate schedule hiding in the data

The most practical reason for the disagreement is the learning-rate schedule. A model planned for 100B tokens, checkpointed at 20B, may still be at a high learning rate; another planned for 20B from the start has finished annealing by then. Both have processed the same tokens, with very different optimization opportunities.

Use the 20B checkpoint as “the loss achievable with 20B tokens,” and the effect of data volume gets mixed up with an unfinished anneal. Hoffmann et al. analyze this schedule mismatch explicitly. What’s actually measured is closer to

L(N,D;h,D,T),L(N,D;h,\mathcal D,\mathcal T),L(N,D;h,D,T),

where hhh includes training method and hyperparameters, D\mathcal DD the data distribution and T\mathcal TT the tokenizer and evaluation. Writing L(N,D)L(N,D)L(N,D) means taking a 2D slice with those fixed or tuned.

The difference compounds. Scale the budget 1000× from the same starting point, and exponent 0.73 gives about 154.9× more parameters while exponent 0.49 gives only about 29.5×. Curves that look similar over a small range extrapolate to configurations several times apart.

Llama 3 takes exactly this lesson: calibrate on your own experiments. The authors ran IsoFLOP sweeps on 40M to 16B models with budgets from 6×10186\times10^{18}6×1018 to 102210^{22}1022 FLOPs. Extrapolated to 3.8×10253.8\times10^{25}3.8×1025, it recommended about 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens, and they chose 405B. They also built a separate mapping from correct-answer negative log-likelihood to task accuracy to predict downstream results (Llama 3 §3.2.1, Figures 2–4).

Three steps need separating. Experiments answer “how does loss change under these conditions?”, optimization answers “where on the surface should the budget land?”, and a final check asks “what does this loss mean for task performance?” Compress that into “bigger is better” and you lose the useful part.

Solving for the optimum under a fixed budget

A parametric fit gives a surface you can differentiate:

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ.L(N,D)=E+\frac{A}{N^\alpha}+\frac{B}{D^\beta}.L(N,D)=E+NαA​+DβB​.

A,B,E,α,βA,B,E,\alpha,\betaA,B,E,α,β are fitted from training runs. EEE is the asymptote; the other two terms are the loss left by a finite model and finite training. This decomposition carries an additivity assumption: too few parameters and too little data cost separately and add up (Chinchilla §3.3, Appendix D.2). We’ll come back to that.

At fixed budget CCC, D=C/(6N)D=C/(6N)D=C/(6N): double the parameters and you halve the tokens. Substituting,

LC(N)=E+AN−α+B(6NC)β.L_C(N)=E+AN^{-\alpha} +B\left(\frac{6N}{C}\right)^\beta.LC​(N)=E+AN−α+B(C6N​)β.

The first term falls as NNN grows; the second rises. The minimum satisfies

αAN−α=βBD−β.\alpha AN^{-\alpha} =\beta BD^{-\beta}.αAN−α=βBD−β.

Both sides are the marginal loss from shifting the same fraction of budget, which is why α\alphaα and β\betaβ can’t be dropped, and why you can’t just set the two loss terms equal. Solving gives

N∗(C)=(αAβB)1/(α+β)(C6)β/(α+β),N^*(C)= \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)} \left(\frac C6\right)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)},N∗(C)=(βBαA​)1/(α+β)(6C​)β/(α+β), D∗(C)=(αAβB)−1/(α+β)(C6)α/(α+β).D^*(C)= \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{-1/(\alpha+\beta)} \left(\frac C6\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}.D∗(C)=(βBαA​)−1/(α+β)(6C​)α/(α+β).

The ratio is hidden in these two expressions. Only when α=β\alpha=\betaα=β do parameters and data both grow as C\sqrt CC​, keeping D/ND/ND/N constant. In general,

D∗N∗∝C(α−β)/(α+β).\frac{D^*}{N^*}\propto C^{(\alpha-\beta)/(\alpha+\beta)}.N∗D∗​∝C(α−β)/(α+β).

“20 tokens per parameter” is a convenient approximation within one set of experiments. The equation doesn’t force it to be constant, and it certainly doesn’t say that’s where to stop training.

Act 2: when the data runs out

So far, every extra unit of DDD was assumed to bring corresponding value. If the unique corpus has only UUU tokens, training KKK epochs processes D=KUD=KUD=KU tokens, but the second epoch’s compute isn’t worth another fresh corpus.

The GPU doesn’t do fewer matmuls on samples it’s seen before, so cost is still about 6NKU6NKU6NKU. What changes is how much loss improvement that extra compute buys. Muennighoff et al. describe the decay with an effective data quantity:

RD=K−1,Deff=U+URD∗(1−e−RD/RD∗).R_D=K-1,\qquad D_{\mathrm{eff}} =U+UR_D^*\left(1-e^{-R_D/R_D^*}\right).RD​=K−1,Deff​=U+URD∗​(1−e−RD​/RD∗​).

The first epoch counts fully as UUU; later repeats have diminishing marginal value, with decay constant RD∗R_D^*RD∗​ estimated from experiments. The paper also constructs an NeffN_{\mathrm{eff}}Neff​ for model sizes beyond what the data ratio needs, and plugs both back into the loss (Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models §3).

Differentiate with respect to actual processed tokens DDD:

∂Deff∂D=e−(K−1)/RD∗.\frac{\partial D_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial D} =e^{-(K-1)/R_D^*}.∂D∂Deff​​=e−(K−1)/RD∗​.

That derivative is easier to reason about than any “maximum epochs” number. It says how many effective tokens one more processed token is worth in the fitted function right now. At K=1+RD∗K=1+R_D^*K=1+RD∗​ it’s still e−1e^{-1}e−1, so nothing suddenly breaks; the marginal value halves at 1+RD∗log⁡21+R_D^*\log21+RD∗​log2.

A finite RD∗R_D^*RD∗​ makes effective data approach U(1+RD∗)U(1+R_D^*)U(1+RD∗​), but that’s not a physical cap on a corpus’s information; change the training method and how well the same data gets used changes too. PaLM’s extended-training experiment is a reminder: extending a 62B model from about 795B to 1.325T tokens, refreshing the corpus beat repeating a sub-corpus, but the refresh also changed mixture proportions and added arXiv and math web pages. The mean of nine English NLP metrics rose from 64.8 to 66.5, and that can’t all be credited to the repetition rate (PaLM Appendix F, Tables 31–33).

Inside the public fitting code

The pinned datablations notebook makes this concrete. The plain surface parameterizes positive coefficients as A=ea,B=eb,E=eeA=e^a,B=e^b,E=e^eA=ea,B=eb,E=ee (the last eee is a log parameter), then computes:

pre_lse = torch.stack([a - alpha*torch.log(inp[:, 0]), b - beta*torch.log(inp[:, 1]), e.expand((inp.shape[0]))])
post_lse = torch.logsumexp(pre_lse, dim=0)
huber_loss = torch.nn.functional.huber_loss(post_lse, torch.log(inp[:, 2]), delta=1e-3, reduction='none')

The prediction is

log⁡L^=LSE⁡(a−αlog⁡N, b−βlog⁡D, e).\log\widehat L= \operatorname{LSE} (a-\alpha\log N,\ b-\beta\log D,\ e).logL=LSE(a−αlogN, b−βlogD, e).

That keeps coefficients positive and computes residuals on log loss. Near the observation, log⁡L^−log⁡L\log\widehat L-\log LlogL−logL approximates the relative error (L^−L)/L(\widehat L-L)/L(L−L)/L, and Huber makes large residuals grow roughly linearly, so a few outliers can’t dominate the fit.

The repeated-data version swaps the effective quantity into the second term:

b - beta * torch.log(inp[:, 1] + (inp[:, 1] * ep_star * (1 - torch.exp(-1*inp[:, 2]/ep_star))))

The two columns are UUU and extra repetitions RDR_DRD​, and ep_star is RD∗R_D^*RD∗​; the parameter term gets the same treatment. With fixed=True, the five base parameters’ gradients are zeroed and only the two decay constants are fitted. That’s staged fitting: repetition value hangs on a previously fitted C4 surface, not a joint re-estimate of seven parameters.

Act 3: when parameters and data interact

Every correction so far kept the additive structure. That has a directly testable consequence. For two unique data budgets U1<U2U_1<U_2U1​<U2​,

L(N,U1)−L(N,U2)=B(U1−β−U2−β),L(N,U_1)-L(N,U_2) =B(U_1^{-\beta}-U_2^{-\beta}),L(N,U1​)−L(N,U2​)=B(U1−β​−U2−β​),

and the right side has no NNN. As the model grows, the loss gap between two data budgets should stay constant.

If the real gap changes systematically with model size, re-estimating five coefficients won’t save it; the surface’s structure has to change. Data-Constrained Language Model Pretraining: Improved Regularization and Scaling Laws studies exactly this. Across five sizes from about 72M to 1.4B, with 100M, 200M, 300M and 400M unique tokens each, the authors built 20 DCLM baseline points, trained on eight 80 GB H100s at context 2048 with epochs and regularization tuned (paper §2, §4, Appendices A–B).

Note the question has changed: how low can loss go with fixed unique data and adequate training, not the fixed-FLOPs question from before. The authors’ SoftQ form is

L(N,U)=E+(ANρ+BUρ/(1+α))α/ρ.L(N,U)=E+ \left( \frac A{N^\rho} +\frac B{U^{\rho/(1+\alpha)}} \right)^{\alpha/\rho}.L(N,U)=E+(NρA​+Uρ/(1+α)B​)α/ρ.

Its α,ρ\alpha,\rhoα,ρ belong to the new model and can’t be mixed with Chinchilla’s. Call the two bracketed terms x,yx,yx,y and the outer exponent qqq; then

∂(x+y)q∂y=q(x+y)q−1.\frac{\partial(x+y)^q}{\partial y}=q(x+y)^{q-1}.∂y∂(x+y)q​=q(x+y)q−1.

As long as q≠1q\ne1q=1, the parameter term xxx changes the data term’s marginal effect. That coupling is exactly what the additive form lacks.

Five held-out points at adjacent sizes support this local structural improvement; extrapolating from 1.4B to hundreds of billions isn’t validated. And you can’t plug an epoch-tuned surface back into 6ND=C6ND=C6ND=C: training length was already optimized away when the surface was built.

This work also uses masked-input regularization, adding an auxiliary loss on masked inputs beyond the clean next-token loss. It improves use of unique data and costs extra compute. “Better data efficiency” has to show that trade: squeezing more training value out of the same corpus isn’t the same as getting more from the same FLOPs.

Act 4: when the model has to be served

Model selection has another real-world condition: how many requests the model will answer after training.

Llama 3’s 405B sits near the authors’ estimated training-compute optimum, while its smaller models were deliberately trained longer. Adding more data to a small model may already be worse than spending the same training compute on a bigger model; but once the small model reaches the needed quality, every future request costs a little less (Llama 3 §1, §3.2.1).

Sardana et al. put inference cost into the selection objective (Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal §2). To compare configurations of equal quality, fix the target loss at ℓ\ellℓ and let III be lifetime inference tokens:

min⁡N,D 6ND+2NIs.t.L(N,D)=ℓ.\min_{N,D}\ 6ND+2NI \quad\text{s.t.}\quad L(N,D)=\ell.N,Dmin​ 6ND+2NIs.t.L(N,D)=ℓ.

The first term is paid once; the second accumulates with use. On the additive loss surface, the data needed to reach the target is

D(N)=(Bℓ−E−AN−α)1/β,N>(Aℓ−E)1/α.D(N)= \left(\frac B{\ell-E-AN^{-\alpha}}\right)^{1/\beta}, \qquad N>\left(\frac A{\ell-E}\right)^{1/\alpha}.D(N)=(ℓ−E−AN−αB​)1/β,N>(ℓ−EA​)1/α.

That lower bound matters: in this function, a small enough model can’t reach the target with infinite training. Near the bound D(N)D(N)D(N) diverges, so smaller isn’t always cheaper.

Differentiating the cost:

J′(N)=6(D+ND′)+2I,J'(N)=6\bigl(D+ND'\bigr)+2I,J′(N)=6(D+ND′)+2I, D′(N)=−D αAN−α−1β(ℓ−E−AN−α).D'(N)= -\frac{D\,\alpha AN^{-\alpha-1}} {\beta(\ell-E-AN^{-\alpha})}.D′(N)=−β(ℓ−E−AN−α)DαAN−α−1​.

With training only, the derivative’s zero is where the first term vanishes. Add a positive III and the derivative there is positive, so shrinking the model slightly lowers total cost, with more training tokens making up the loss. The shift toward smaller models comes from the equal-quality constraint, not from assuming small models are better.

At the high-demand end the recommendation is extremely long training, exactly where loss extrapolation fails first. Sardana et al. trained 47 MPT models from 150M to 6B on non-repeated web and code data; the extreme 10,000 tokens/parameter ratio was only tried at 150M, without full coverage at larger sizes. They also found that curves fitted at conventional ratios overestimate the benefit of extremely long training (same paper §3–5).

So this figure explains why training small models longer is worth it, without guaranteeing a specific product can skip validation in the very-long-training regime. And if Act 2’s unique-data constraint also applies, the “fresh data is available” assumption has to change again.

Real money also depends on actual serving. Prefill processes inputs at once and decode generates token by token, and equal FLOPs can run at different utilization because of batching, KV cache and bandwidth limits. A more deployment-shaped objective is

Cost=ctrain(N,D)+Q E[cprefill(N,P)+cdecode(N,P,T)],\mathrm{Cost} =c_{\mathrm{train}}(N,D) +Q\,\mathbb E[ c_{\mathrm{prefill}}(N,P) +c_{\mathrm{decode}}(N,P,T)],Cost=ctrain​(N,D)+QE[cprefill​(N,P)+cdecode​(N,P,T)],

with QQQ requests and input/output lengths P,TP,TP,T. These cost functions have to be measured on the target hardware, parallel configuration and latency requirements. A model that just shrinks enough to fit on one GPU may drop a price tier; saved FLOPs don’t necessarily translate into savings at the same ratio.

Act 5: when inference itself is a choice

Inference volume III isn’t necessarily a given constant either. The same question can be answered once, or with multiple candidates for a verifier to choose from, or with a revision after reading the first answer. The strategy decides how many tokens get spent and also changes the chance of being right.

Call that strategy π\piπ, and the objective becomes

max⁡N,D,πEx[Score(x;N,D,π)]s.t.6ND+Q Ex[c(x;N,π)]≤Ctotal.\max_{N,D,\pi} \mathbb E_x[\mathrm{Score}(x;N,D,\pi)] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad 6ND+Q\,\mathbb E_x[c(x;N,\pi)] \le C_{\mathrm{total}}.N,D,πmax​Ex​[Score(x;N,D,π)]s.t.6ND+QEx​[c(x;N,π)]≤Ctotal​.

This doesn’t produce a new universal recipe, but it adds the missing decision variable back. Pretraining cross-entropy alone can’t decide whether a correct answer is among the candidates, let alone whether a verifier can pick it.

Snell et al., on MATH’s 12k-train/500-test split, used PaLM 2-S* models trained for revision or verification to compare sequential revision and candidate search, and found the right budget allocation differs by difficulty. Estimating difficulty with 2048 samples per question has its own cost, which the paper doesn’t fully include in its main efficiency analysis (paper §3–4).

On easy questions near saturation, ten times more candidates may just burn compute; on very hard ones where correct solutions are almost never sampled, an evaluator can’t choose a candidate that doesn’t exist. Allocating budget in practice requires estimating which requests still benefit from more compute, using only information available at serving time. The self-correction, selection and process supervision post goes into these differences in detail.

Conclusion

Back to the opening: 7B on 140B tokens, or 70B on 14B? If the only question is validation loss at fixed training FLOPs, solve along the Chinchilla surface and you’re done. Each new condition moves the answer:

  • Finite data devalues repeated tokens, and the fitted effective-data curve can’t express overfitting.
  • Coupled parameters and data break the additive surface’s prediction that data gaps don’t depend on model size.
  • Deployment pushes equal-quality choices toward smaller, longer-trained models, until extrapolation stops being trustworthy.
  • Inference-time strategy makes part of the compute budget a choice in its own right.

Clinging to one tokens-per-parameter ratio throws away exactly what scaling laws are for, which is making these decisions.

A scaling law isn’t a recipe; it’s a way to compute the recipe for the conditions you actually have.

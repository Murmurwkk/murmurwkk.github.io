Swapping Gaussian for Student-t Changes More Than the Tail

Hand a Gaussian's Q to a Student-t with 4 degrees of freedom and its covariance doubles. Scale, central pull and tail all change at once; untangling which one moved a robust WiFi solution, failures included.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 12 个章节
#Student-t#robust estimation#radio positioning#covariance#numerical optimization

“Our WiFi positions have outliers, so let’s use a Student-t instead of a Gaussian.” It’s one of the most common robustness moves in estimation, and it sounds like a one-line change: keep the covariance QQQ, swap the distribution.

Here’s the first surprise. Hand a 2D Gaussian’s QQQ unchanged to a Student-t with 4 degrees of freedom, and the resulting distribution has covariance 2Q2Q2Q. You meant to change the tail, and you doubled the scale.

Fine, then compare against a Gaussian with covariance 2Q2Q2Q. That matches the covariance, but now the center doesn’t match: in the original whitened coordinates, Gaussian 2Q pulls with a constant gradient coefficient of 1/21/21/2, while the Student-t starts at 3/23/23/2 and decays. Same observations, different stationary trajectories.

On a real flat-floor walking log (T31), this matters. In snapshot A, Student-t cuts the position error dramatically; in snapshot C, it’s worse than both Gaussians. In this post I want to untangle why. We’ll build up the fundamentals first (scale vs. covariance, influence functions, probability regions), then go to the flat-floor snapshots and look at one observation whose weight collapses to 0.14. After that we’ll run an equal-budget control that changes the picture for B and C, make sure the solver is optimizing what we think it is, and close with why normalization constants can flip a model ranking.

Fundamentals: scale matrix vs. covariance

Work in whitened coordinates throughout. With residual e=pEN+a−ze=p_{EN}+a-ze=pEN​+a−z and Q=LL⊤Q=LL^\topQ=LL⊤:

r=L−1e,s=r⊤r=e⊤Q−1e.r=L^{-1}e,\qquad s=r^\top r=e^\top Q^{-1}e.r=L−1e,s=r⊤r=e⊤Q−1e.

eee is in meters, QQQ in m², rrr and sss are dimensionless. Staying in the original QQQ‘s coordinates when comparing likelihoods matters, so that “two standard deviations” means the same thing on every plot.

The Gaussian Q negative log-density is

ℓG(e;Q)=log⁡(2π)+12log⁡∣Q∣+12s,\ell_G(e;Q)=\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|Q|+\frac12s,ℓG​(e;Q)=log(2π)+21​log∣Q∣+21​s,

where QQQ is the covariance. The 2D Student-t is

pt(e;ν,S)=Γ((ν+2)/2)Γ(ν/2) νπ ∣S∣1/2(1+e⊤S−1eν)−(ν+2)/2,p_t(e;\nu,S)= \frac{\Gamma((\nu+2)/2)}{\Gamma(\nu/2)\,\nu\pi\,|S|^{1/2}} \left(1+\frac{e^\top S^{-1}e}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+2)/2},pt​(e;ν,S)=Γ(ν/2)νπ∣S∣1/2Γ((ν+2)/2)​(1+νe⊤S−1e​)−(ν+2)/2,

where SSS is a scale matrix, not a covariance. For ν>2\nu>2ν>2,

Cov⁡(e)=νν−2S.\operatorname{Cov}(e)=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}S.Cov(e)=ν−2ν​S.

At ν=4\nu=4ν=4 and S=QS=QS=Q, that’s 2Q2Q2Q. The Student-t filtering literature makes this point repeatedly; Roth et al. also show that matching only the covariance makes the substitute distribution’s center too peaked (paper, §2.1–2.2, §4.3).

Writing S=cQS=cQS=cQ, the 2D Gamma factors cancel, leaving a convenient unified form:

ℓt(e;ν,cQ)=log⁡(2π)+12log⁡∣Q∣+log⁡c+ν+22log⁡(1+sνc).\ell_t(e;\nu,cQ) =\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|Q|+\log c +\frac{\nu+2}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{s}{\nu c}\right).ℓt​(e;ν,cQ)=log(2π)+21​log∣Q∣+logc+2ν+2​log(1+νcs​).

Fundamentals: who pulls how hard

The gradient tells you how hard each observation pulls on the state. Dropping constants,

∇rℓt=w(s)r,w(s)=ν+2νc+s.\nabla_r\ell_t=w(s)r, \qquad w(s)=\frac{\nu+2}{\nu c+s}.∇r​ℓt​=w(s)r,w(s)=νc+sν+2​.

Gaussian Q has w=1w=1w=1, Gaussian 2Q has w=1/2w=1/2w=1/2. The historical Student-t has

w(s)=64+s.w(s)=\frac6{4+s}.w(s)=4+s6​.

Trace it out: w=1.5w=1.5w=1.5 at s=0s=0s=0, equal to Gaussian Q’s 1 at s=2s=2s=2, and not down to Gaussian 2Q’s 0.5 until s=8s=8s=8. So the Student-t pulls harder than either Gaussian on small residuals, and only lets go on large ones. “Robust” doesn’t mean “softer everywhere.”

It’s also worth being clear about what www isn’t. It isn’t the probability that an observation is trustworthy; it can exceed 1, and it depends on the current state. If the initial trajectory is far off, a genuinely good observation shows up as a large residual and gets down-weighted. A cluster of mutually consistent observations that all sit on the wrong position island keeps large weights together. Student-t doesn’t decide which mode is real for you.

The same weight falls out of the Gaussian scale-mixture view:

λ∼Gamma⁡(ν/2,ν/2),e∣λ∼N(0,cQ/λ),\lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2), \qquad e\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal N(0,cQ/\lambda),λ∼Gamma(ν/2,ν/2),e∣λ∼N(0,cQ/λ),

(second Gamma parameter is a rate), so that given the residual,

λ∣e∼Gamma⁡(ν+22,ν+s/c2),E[λ∣e]c=ν+2νc+s.\lambda\mid e\sim \operatorname{Gamma}\left(\frac{\nu+2}{2},\frac{\nu+s/c}{2}\right), \qquad \frac{\mathbb E[\lambda\mid e]}c=\frac{\nu+2}{\nu c+s}.λ∣e∼Gamma(2ν+2​,2ν+s/c​),cE[λ∣e]​=νc+sν+2​.

That’s where “precision adapts to the residual” comes from. It doesn’t mean every solver must estimate λ\lambdaλ; this project computes the exact marginal negative log-likelihood directly and produces no calibrated fault probability.

Case A: one observation lets go

Let’s look at how the Student-t weighted A’s six radio observations. The saved radio_likelihood has each residual and Q, so we can compute directly

si=ei⊤Qi−1ei,wi=64+si,s_i=e_i^\top Q_i^{-1}e_i, \qquad w_i=\frac6{4+s_i},si​=ei⊤​Qi−1​ei​,wi​=4+si​6​,

and check against the record.

This is the mechanism in miniature. The Student-t didn’t fit this observation better; it left a larger residual, 6.247 m instead of 4.406 m, while cutting its pull to 0.14. The other five observations in A got weights of about 1.009–1.439, well above 2Q’s flat 0.5. So the trajectory could honor the other observations and the Motion chain more tightly, without paying full price for one conflicting term.

Is the 266 s observation therefore “wrong”? Not necessarily. The conflict could come from Motion bias, a wrong position mode, timing or unmodeled correlation. The weight only knows the residual under the current joint model; it doesn’t know whose fault it is.

D makes a useful contrast. Its most down-weighted observation, at 390 s, has s≈8.324s\approx8.324s≈8.324 and w≈0.487w\approx0.487w≈0.487, about the same as Gaussian 2Q. There are weighting differences on smaller residuals, but the final reference errors differ by only about 1.6 cm. “Heavy tails are more robust” is too coarse a story. What actually predicts the effect is whether there’s a sharply conflicting observation for the weights to redistribute away from.

Making the solver optimize the right thing

So far we’ve assumed the solver minimizes the Student-t objective. It’s worth checking, because the standard way of feeding a robust loss to a least-squares solver has a classic pitfall.

The historical implementation uses a radial residual transform:

ρ(s)=(ν+2)log⁡(1+s/ν),g(r)=a(s)r,a(s)=ρ(s)s,\rho(s)=(\nu+2)\log(1+s/\nu), \qquad g(r)=a(s)r, \qquad a(s)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho(s)}s},ρ(s)=(ν+2)log(1+s/ν),g(r)=a(s)r,a(s)=sρ(s)​​,

so that 12g⊤g=12ρ(s)\tfrac12g^\top g=\tfrac12\rho(s)21​g⊤g=21​ρ(s), exactly the Student-t negative log-likelihood without constants. At s→0s\to0s→0, a(0)=(ν+2)/νa(0)=\sqrt{(\nu+2)/\nu}a(0)=(ν+2)/ν​, and the code switches to a series expansion near zero to avoid cancellation.

The pitfall: g = weight * r looks like weighted least squares, but weight depends on rrr. The full Jacobian is

Jg=aI+2a′(s)rr⊤,2a′(s)=ρ′(s)s−ρ(s)a(s)s2,J_g=aI+2a'(s)rr^\top, \qquad 2a'(s)=\frac{\rho'(s)s-\rho(s)}{a(s)s^2},Jg​=aI+2a′(s)rr⊤,2a′(s)=a(s)s2ρ′(s)s−ρ(s)​,

and only with that second term do you get the right gradient,

Jg⊤g=ρ′(s)r=ν+2ν+sr.J_g^\top g=\rho'(s)r=\frac{\nu+2}{\nu+s}r.Jg⊤​g=ρ′(s)r=ν+sν+2​r.

Three approaches often all get called “weighted least squares,” and they compute different things:

ApproachWhat it actually computes
Radial transform g, with full JgJ_gJg​Exact Student-t objective and exact first-order gradient, followed by a Gauss–Newton approximation
IRLS with w(s)=ρ′(s)w(s)=\rho'(s)w(s)=ρ′(s), w fixed each roundAn iterative quadratic surrogate whose gradient matches at the current point; weights must be updated and descent checked against the original objective
g=a(s)rg=a(s)rg=a(s)r but dropping the derivative of aNeither of the above; usually doesn’t even match the original objective’s first-order gradient

The historical code does the first, correctly: in assemble it replaces only the Radio residual rows (two per observation) and left-multiplies the original Jacobian by the full 2D derivative. Motion and pressure rows are untouched.

Exact Hessian vs. Gauss–Newton

Getting the gradient right still doesn’t make the Gauss–Newton matrix the true Hessian. In whitened coordinates for ν=4,S=Q\nu=4,S=Qν=4,S=Q,

Hr=64+sI−12(4+s)2rr⊤.H_r=\frac6{4+s}I-\frac{12}{(4+s)^2}rr^\top.Hr​=4+s6​I−(4+s)212​rr⊤.

Perpendicular to the residual the eigenvalue is 6/(4+s)6/(4+s)6/(4+s); along it,

λ∥=6(4−s)(4+s)2.\lambda_{\parallel}=\frac{6(4-s)}{(4+s)^2}.λ∥​=(4+s)26(4−s)​.

That goes negative once s>4s>4s>4 (whitened radius above 2): the influence function is falling off, so the loss is locally concave in the radial direction. Gauss–Newton can’t see this. Jg⊤JgJ_g^\top J_gJg⊤​Jg​ is always positive semidefinite, with radial eigenvalue

λ∥GN=(ρ′(s)a(s))2.\lambda_{\parallel}^{GN}=\left(\frac{\rho'(s)}{a(s)}\right)^2.λ∥GN​=(a(s)ρ′(s)​)2.

A concrete point makes the gap vivid. At s=25s=25s=25, the true radial curvature is −0.149822, the GN curvature is 0.090035 and the frozen IRLS weight is 0.206897. Same model, three numbers, three different objects.

For the full state, the chain rule adds the residual’s own second derivative:

Hθ=Jr⊤HrJr+∑j(∇rℓ)j ∇θ2rj.H_\theta =J_r^\top H_rJ_r +\sum_j (\nabla_r\ell)_j\,\nabla_\theta^2r_j.Hθ​=Jr⊤​Hr​Jr​+j∑​(∇r​ℓ)j​∇θ2​rj​.

Other factors can keep the total objective positive definite at a stationary point, so a negative single-factor curvature doesn’t doom the optimization. But if you invert a local matrix to report uncertainty, you need to say whether it’s GN, the true local Laplace curvature or something else, and check where it applies. Solver damping isn’t extra physical precision. And one level further down: a Gaussian Motion times a Student-t Radio doesn’t give a clean Student-t posterior. A robust MAP fit isn’t an exact Student-t filter.

Comparing models: normalization constants matter

Within one model, state-independent constants don’t affect the solution, so solvers drop them. Across models, they come back. In 2D with scale Q, the Gaussian and every Student-t happen to share the same normalization,

CQ=log⁡(2π)+12log⁡∣Q∣,C_Q=\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|Q|,CQ​=log(2π)+21​log∣Q∣,

but Gaussian 2Q adds log⁡2\log2log2 per observation. A has 6 Radio observations, so that’s 6log⁡2≈4.1588836\log2\approx4.1588836log2≈4.158883, and in the saved results it’s enough to flip the ranking:

This still isn’t Bayesian model evidence: it evaluates each objective at its own selected parameters, without integrating over the parameter posterior or paying for complexity or selection.

The honest generalization test is to freeze the fit and compare normalized predictive densities on observations that didn’t take part in construction:

p(znew∣D,M)=∫p(znew∣θ,M) p(θ∣D,M) dθ.p(z_{new}\mid D,M) =\int p(z_{new}\mid\theta,M)\,p(\theta\mid D,M)\,d\theta.p(znew​∣D,M)=∫p(znew​∣θ,M)p(θ∣D,M)dθ.

And that needs the full observation definition: picking the one position island a later algorithm chose and calling it “independent radio truth” changes the question. That’s the subject of the frozen prediction post.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows that on A, residual-dependent weighting reduced one conflicting observation’s pull and produced a solution that uniformly enlarging the covariance couldn’t reach.
  • It shows that the same redistribution can hurt: C regresses under Student-t, and under an equal budget B’s Student-t didn’t converge at all.
  • It shows the historical Student-t loss and its Jacobian are implemented correctly, locally and on the full graph.
  • It doesn’t identify the true degrees of freedom of the radio noise, or show better cross-log prediction. There are no confidence intervals, and B/C have one reference point each.
  • The four snapshots overlap and share a log; they’re not independent trials, and four good segments don’t make a continuous trajectory.
  • A/D still follow the historical protocol and haven’t been rerun under the equal budget.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. A Student-t with S=QS=QS=Q isn’t “Gaussian Q with fatter tails.” It has twice the covariance, 1.5× the central curvature and a radial curvature that turns negative beyond two whitened sigmas. Matching any one of those leaves the others different. In the flat-floor walk, that combination helped dramatically where one observation sharply conflicted with the rest (A), did nothing much where none did (D), and hurt or failed to converge elsewhere (B, C).

For the next round, I’d fix the matching condition first and then freeze scale, initialization and stopping rules for every model. Equal-covariance and equal-curvature controls should be separate, pre-registered arms. Model selection should happen on new logs or held-out observations, not on the same few sparse reference points.

Swapping a Gaussian for a Student-t changes the tail, the scale and the curvature at once; a good experiment says which one it’s testing.

Appendix B: related work on adaptive scale

Roth et al.’s analysis of exact Student-t recursions shows that once process and measurement noise are propagated, multiplied and conditioned, a simple recursion requires explicit approximation; their filter uses a joint t approximation with scale adjustment. That’s a different level from swapping the loss on a few position factors (paper, §3.3–4.3).

Deng et al. model both prediction error and measurement likelihood as Student-t, update scales with an inverse-Wishart prior and variational iterations, and model the selection process of whether an observation is transmitted. Their validation is a 2D constant-velocity simulation: 4D state, 2D position observations, process and measurement noise with 100× covariance at probabilities 5%/10%, 10,000 runs of 200 steps, 5 degrees of freedom. An RST-KF that knows the exact noise and receives every observation still beats the new method, so the information conditions differ (Sensors 2025, §4–5).

The most useful idea from that work is to separate three questions: does the tail shape fit, is the base scale miscalibrated, and do missing observations carry selection information? It doesn’t validate T31, and multimodal radio positions aren’t that simulation’s position measurements. Any adaptive-scale mechanism added later needs its own control, so the total gain doesn’t get credited to Student-t by default.

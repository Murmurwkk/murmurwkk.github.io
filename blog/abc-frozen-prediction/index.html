Held-Out Data That Isn't Independent

Holding out later observations is the right instinct, but in navigation every segment shares the same inertial, map and device errors. How to freeze a candidate correction and still score it honestly.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 10 个章节
#integrated navigation#conditional probability#sequential testing#experiment audit

Suppose a stretch of WiFi observations supports some correction to your trajectory. A few seconds later, a new batch arrives. If you refit the candidate with the new batch included, the score goes up. Great, except that the new batch has now helped choose the model, so it can no longer tell you whether the model was right.

Anyone who’s done machine learning knows the fix: hold data out. Build the candidate on segments A and B, freeze it, and use segment C only to score a prediction. That’s the right instinct, and it’s what this post is about.

But navigation has a wrinkle that a random train/test split doesn’t. A, B and C can share the same inertial error, the same fingerprint map and the same device’s RSS bias. They don’t overlap in time, and they’re still not independent. Separation in time is not statistical independence.

We’ll start with a tiny example of why, then build the protocol piece by piece: checking that A and B agree, deciding exactly what gets frozen, scoring C by integrating over the state, keeping the score ledger honest, and finally asking what it would take for this score to be a real sequential test. The running example is a frozen candidate from a flat-floor walking log (T31).

Fundamentals: time separation isn’t independence

Here’s the core problem in two equations. Two observations share a common bias bbb:

z1=x1+b+ϵ1,z2=x2+b+ϵ2.z_1=x_1+b+\epsilon_1,\qquad z_2=x_2+b+\epsilon_2.z1​=x1​+b+ϵ1​,z2​=x2​+b+ϵ2​.

Even if the noises ϵ1\epsilon_1ϵ1​ and ϵ2\epsilon_2ϵ2​ are independent, z1z_1z1​ and z2z_2z2​ share uncertainty as long as bbb is unknown. Taking the second observation ten seconds later doesn’t remove bbb. Fingerprint-map errors, device RSS bias, initial attitude and overlapping inertial inputs all behave like bbb.

In the linear-Gaussian case the joint distribution is

[z1z2]∼N ⁣([μ1μ2],[S11S12S21S22]),\begin{bmatrix}z_1\\z_2\end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\!\left( \begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix} \right),[z1​z2​​]∼N([μ1​μ2​​],[S11​S21​​S12​S22​​]),

and once z1z_1z1​ is known, the right prediction for z2z_2z2​ is the conditional

μ2∣1=μ2+S21S11−1(z1−μ1),S2∣1=S22−S21S11−1S12.\mu_{2\mid1}=\mu_2+S_{21}S_{11}^{-1}(z_1-\mu_1),\qquad S_{2\mid1}=S_{22}-S_{21}S_{11}^{-1}S_{12}.μ2∣1​=μ2​+S21​S11−1​(z1​−μ1​),S2∣1​=S22​−S21​S11−1​S12​.

(In code, solve the linear system; don’t form the inverse.) Notice that conditioning moves the mean, not just the variance. That’s why correlation isn’t a uniform “discount factor”: a new observation that moves along the shared bias may be less surprising than it looks, while one that breaks the shared pattern may be more surprising.

Let’s put numbers on it. Common-bias variance 4, both noise variances 1, with the known x1,x2x_1,x_2x1​,x2​ removed:

S=[5445].S=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{bmatrix}.S=[54​45​].

Observe a first residual of 2. The second residual’s prediction becomes mean 4/5×2=1.64/5\times2=1.64/5×2=1.6, variance 5−16/5=1.85-16/5=1.85−16/5=1.8. Ignore the first observation and you’d predict mean 0, variance 5.

That’s neither “halve the second observation’s weight” nor “throw the second observation away.” The first observation helped identify the shared bias, so the second should be tested around the updated prediction. Hold onto this picture; the rest of the post is this idea applied carefully.

The protocol: build on A and B, freeze, predict C

Figure 1: Freeze pins down both the construction result and the scoring rule. Common uncertainty still flows across every stage. C is only scored; it never flows back to pick a candidate.

Some vocabulary. KEEP is the existing explanation. A candidate correction is described by a parameter θ\thetaθ: a translation, a finite rotation, or a change to a stretch of state constraints. A, B and C are segments assigned by protocol, not places on a map. (Other experiments have snapshots called A–D; those are unrelated.)

Step 1: do A and B agree?

Construction starts by estimating θ^A\hat\theta_Aθ^A​ and θ^B\hat\theta_Bθ^B​ separately and checking that they’re compatible. The difference d=θ^A−θ^Bd=\hat\theta_A-\hat\theta_Bd=θ^A​−θ^B​ has covariance

Cov⁡(d)=PA+PB−PAB−PABT.\operatorname{Cov}(d)=P_A+P_B-P_{AB}-P_{AB}^{T}.Cov(d)=PA​+PB​−PAB​−PABT​.

Drop the cross term and you’ve silently assumed it’s zero, which is exactly the assumption our toy example warned against. Two failure modes follow:

  • A shared error drags both segments toward the same wrong answer. They agree beautifully, which shows they’re compatible, not that they’re right.
  • Forcing independence miscomputes the uncertainty of ddd, making the check too loose or too strict, depending on the correlation.

Step 2: build the joint candidate without double counting

If A and B pass, they’re combined into one joint candidate that uses all the construction information. The trap here is double counting: two posteriors that already contain the same initial prior can’t be fused as if they were two independent measurements. Either go back to the original factors and the common source, or use an equivalent representation where the conditioning set and cross-covariances are explicit. “Average the two posteriors” skips the one question that matters.

Step 3: freeze

What gets frozen is more than the candidate’s numbers. At minimum:

  • candidate parameters and their construction sources;
  • how the state posterior is approximated;
  • the noise model, mode weights and the NULL explanation;
  • how correlated observations are grouped, and observation bindings;
  • the numerical integration rule and the candidate selection policy;
  • the risk budget, if there is one.

In this case, the three original WiFi modes and their masses are kept as-is rather than averaged into one center. The noise is a Student-t with ν=4\nu=4ν=4, using the provider’s QQQ as the scale matrix, so the single-mode covariance is 2Q2Q2Q. The NULL branch keeps its frozen weight and density, with no recalibration against later ground truth.

Freezing doesn’t make any of these choices correct. What it buys is that C tests the same claim that was written down before C arrived, instead of a claim whose interpretation shifted with the results. If the noise model is wrong, freezing makes that visible rather than hiding it.

Scoring C: integrate over the state, not the mean

The frozen likelihood for the iii-th observation is a mixture of Student-t modes plus a NULL background:

gi(xi)=(1−πi)∑kwik t4(zik−xi;Qi)+πici,g_i(x_i)=(1-\pi_i)\sum_k w_{ik}\, t_4(z_{ik}-x_i;Q_i)+\pi_i c_i,gi​(xi​)=(1−πi​)k∑​wik​t4​(zik​−xi​;Qi​)+πi​ci​,

with mode masses wikw_{ik}wik​, NULL weight πi\pi_iπi​ and background density cic_ici​. The prediction score integrates over state uncertainty rather than plugging in the trajectory mean:

q(C1,C2)=∫g1(x1)g2(x2)N(x;μ,P) dx.q(C_1,C_2)=\int g_1(x_1)g_2(x_2) \mathcal N(x;\mu,P)\,dx.q(C1​,C2​)=∫g1​(x1​)g2​(x2​)N(x;μ,P)dx.

That product g1(x1)g2(x2)g_1(x_1)g_2(x_2)g1​(x1​)g2​(x2​) hides an assumption worth stating: given the states, the two observation mechanisms factor. Keeping the state cross-covariance doesn’t model any correlated error left between the two scans; if a shared device bias isn’t in the state, the product can still be wrong. That’s an assumption of the frozen model, and no amount of integration accuracy can prove it.

A trick for the integral

Integrating a Student-t mixture against a 4D Gaussian sounds expensive. The trick is the Gaussian scale-mixture form of the Student-t. For a 2D observation with ν=4\nu=4ν=4:

λ∼Gamma⁡(2,2),e∣λ∼N(0,Q/λ),\lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(2,2),\qquad e\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal N(0,Q/\lambda),λ∼Gamma(2,2),e∣λ∼N(0,Q/λ),

with the second Gamma parameter a rate. Conditioned on each λi\lambda_iλi​, the state integral is just a Gaussian convolution: add Qi/λiQ_i/\lambda_iQi​/λi​ blockwise to the joint state covariance PPP and evaluate a multivariate Gaussian density at the observed mode positions. NULL branches have constant density and only need the remaining live state dimensions marginalized. So you enumerate mode combinations and do a 1D quadrature per λ\lambdaλ, instead of gridding 4D position space.

The historical implementation used adaptive Gauss–Kronrod. The independent recomputation uses generalized Gauss–Laguerre instead: with u=2λu=2\lambdau=2λ the weight is ue−uu e^{-u}ue−u, i.e. α=1\alpha=1α=1. It was run at 16, 32, 64 and 128 nodes without ever calling the historical scorer:

# For each non-NULL mode combination; Q is the Student-t scale matrix
nodes, weights = roots_genlaguerre(order, alpha=1)
precision = nodes / 2
covariance = state_joint_covariance + block_diag(Q1 / lam1, Q2 / lam2)
value += mixture_weight * quadrature_weight * gaussian_density(
    observed_mode_positions, state_mean, covariance
)

The ledger: why the first score stays on the books

Now back to drifting estimates. Mark each information cutoff with a subscript. What this case accumulates is

S2=log⁡qt1(C1)+log⁡qt2(C1,C2)qt2(C1).S_2=\log q_{t_1}(C_1) +\log\frac{q_{t_2}(C_1,C_2)}{q_{t_2}(C_1)}.S2​=logqt1​​(C1​)+logqt2​​(C1​)qt2​​(C1​,C2​)​.

The first term was computed and frozen at t1t_1t1​. The second is C2’s conditional density under the common prediction at t2t_2t2​. Replace the first term with log⁡qt2(C1)\log q_{t_2}(C_1)logqt2​​(C1​) and the sum becomes the later cutoff’s joint log-density, which looks tidier but has silently rewritten the first entry in the ledger with information that didn’t exist yet.

Figure 3: Frozen sequential total vs. later joint density. The gap of about 0.146236 is not floating-point error; the first term was computed under different information. At the later cutoff, conditional C2 scores about 0.540286 higher than C2’s marginal alone.

That 0.540286 is our toy example again. Keeping the correlation structure changes how surprising C2 is. Meanwhile the 0.146236 is the cost of rewriting history. “Rescore everything against the latest trajectory and add it up” makes exactly this mistake: it either reuses old observations or pretends later information was known earlier.

One more caution: there’s no rule like “more negative score, worse candidate.” Continuous densities carry units, so absolute log-densities across different observation spaces aren’t comparable. To compare a candidate with KEEP, build both predictive distributions on the same observations, the same base measure and the same available information.

From a score to a test

It’s tempting to go one step further and turn this score into a stopping rule. When both hypotheses H1H_1H1​ and H0H_0H0​ define valid one-step conditional distributions, you can form

Lt=∏i=1tp1(zi∣Fi−1)p0(zi∣Fi−1).L_t=\prod_{i=1}^{t} \frac{p_1(z_i\mid\mathcal F_{i-1})}{p_0(z_i\mid\mathcal F_{i-1})}.Lt​=i=1∏t​p0​(zi​∣Fi−1​)p1​(zi​∣Fi−1​)​.

If support, conditional-model and predictable-selection requirements all hold, a simple null gives E0[Lt∣Ft−1]=Lt−1\mathbb E_0[L_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=L_{t-1}E0​[Lt​∣Ft−1​]=Lt−1​, and the crossing control of a non-negative (super)martingale tells you when stopping is allowed. Even then, if the next message is a deterministic copy of the last one, it carries nothing new, and its likelihood ratio can’t be multiplied in again.

Real navigation rarely meets those conditions. Variances are estimated, candidates are pre-selected, mode masses are empirical and the background distribution is only partly known. A variable named log_score that keeps improving satisfies none of the requirements by virtue of its name. With a composite null, the property must hold across the entire specified class of null distributions, not just in expectation under one fitted reference model.

📝 Further reading: Sequential testing of conditionally constrained hypotheses studies hypothesis classes defined by finitely many conditional constraints, and products of predictable one-step e-variables. It reinforces how much the conditional model and predictable selection matter, and its theorems explicitly don’t let you wrap an arbitrary correlated score into a safe sequential test.1

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows that the conditional integral and the frozen score accounting are numerically consistent: two independent quadrature methods agree to about 6.85×10−106.85\times10^{-10}6.85×10−10 in log-density.
  • It shows, concretely, how much the correlation structure (0.540286) and ledger rewriting (0.146236) change the score in one real case.
  • There’s no full KEEP comparison under equal conditions and no risk calibration, so there’s no measured false-alarm rate for any online threshold.
  • The factorization of the two observation mechanisms given the state, the mode masses and the NULL density are assumptions of the frozen model, not verified facts.
  • A new front-end replay, the full nonlinear posterior, the asynchronous source timing and runtime commits are outside what was verified.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. Held-out segments are still the right idea in navigation, but they share errors, so every step has to carry the cross-covariance. That covers the compatibility check between A and B, the joint construction, and the prediction for C. Freezing pins down rules, not truth. Each observation has several timestamps, and only the availability boundary says what was known when. Scores are integrated over state uncertainty, and the ledger keeps each entry under the information it was computed with.

In practice that means recording each score’s model version, information cutoff and observation sources. Non-converged numerics get recorded as “unresolved.” A reused common prior means fixing the joint model. And if you looked at C and then changed the candidate, you re-split construction and test data. Each of these guards against a different statistical error.

In the end the whole protocol reduces to one question: before seeing this C, could the candidate’s prediction have been written down? If the frozen object is that well defined, C can test it. If not, C just becomes part of yet another fit.

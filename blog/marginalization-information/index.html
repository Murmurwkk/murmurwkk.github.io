What Marginalization Remembers, Including the Mistakes

Marginalization keeps old constraints after old states are dropped, wrong ones included. Schur and QR from a hand-solvable example, 700 replayed propagations, and what a sliding window costs.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 13 个章节
#marginalization#common covariance#square-root form#tail latency#navigation experiments

Every sliding-window estimator faces the same tension. The window has to drop old states to stay bounded, but the constraints those old observations placed on the current states are worth keeping. Marginalization resolves it: rewrite the joint problem over old and current variables as a prior over the current ones alone.

There’s a catch, and it’s the theme of this post. Keeping information also keeps mistakes. Once a wrong old position observation has been folded into the compressed prior, deleting its raw record doesn’t undo it. The prior remembers, even when you’d rather it didn’t.

The post has two parts. Part 1 is the math. We’ll start with a two-variable example you can do by hand, derive marginalization as a Schur complement and then as QR on the Jacobian, and look at what freezing a linearization point means. Then we’ll see why nine numbers per node isn’t the whole state, and replay 700 real front-end propagations to check the bookkeeping. Part 2 is the systems side: where a 31.3 GiB matrix comes from, whether each 500 ms window finishes in time, and whether a background candidate can ever catch up with the foreground.

Part 1: the math

A toy you can do by hand

All variances are 1. An old state xxx has prior x∼N(0,1)x\sim N(0,1)x∼N(0,1). A motion relation says y−x≈1y-x\approx1y−x≈1. And there’s a wrong old position observation, x≈10x\approx10x≈10. The three residuals are xxx, y−x−1y-x-1y−x−1 and x−10x-10x−10, which gives

H=[3−1−11],g=[91].H=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad g=\begin{bmatrix}9\\1\end{bmatrix}.H=[3−1​−11​],g=[91​].

Eliminate xxx and you get H′=2/3H'=2/3H′=2/3, g′=4g'=4g′=4, so the marginal for yyy is N(6,1.5)N(6,1.5)N(6,1.5). If instead you had removed the wrong observation first and then eliminated xxx, you’d get N(1,2)N(1,2)N(1,2). Different mean, different variance.

Figure 1: Deletion order matters. Removing the wrong observation before compression gives N(1,2); compressing first gives N(6,1.5).

Now a new, independent observation y≈0y\approx0y≈0 with variance 1 arrives. The model carrying the wrong history gives a mean of 2.4; the model without it gives 1/31/31/3. New information does revise old conclusions, but that’s not the same as the old error being undone.

And here’s the real point. Once xxx and its factors have been released, deleting a record labelled “old position observation” won’t turn N(6,1.5)N(6,1.5)N(6,1.5) back into N(1,2)N(1,2)N(1,2). Subtracting the original JfTJfJ_f^TJ_fJfT​Jf​ usually isn’t enough either, since it acted on a variable that’s already been eliminated, and the Schur complement involves the whole coupled system. Exact undo requires keeping enough recomputable history or a retractable factorization. Keeping the observation’s name isn’t enough.

Marginalization is a Schur complement

Let’s derive what just happened. Near a fixed linearization point, call the increments to be removed δm\delta_mδm​ and the ones kept δr\delta_rδr​. Use the convention

E(δ)=12δTHδ−gTδ+c,Hδ=g.E(\delta)=\frac12\delta^T H\delta-g^T\delta+c, \qquad H\delta=g.E(δ)=21​δTHδ−gTδ+c,Hδ=g.

(From whitened residuals 12∥r+Jδ∥2\frac12\|r+J\delta\|^221​∥r+Jδ∥2, that’s H=JTJH=J^TJH=JTJ and g=−JTrg=-J^Trg=−JTr; from 12∥Aδ−b∥2\frac12\|A\delta-b\|^221​∥Aδ−b∥2, it’s g=ATbg=A^Tbg=ATb. Both are fine; just don’t mix right-hand-side signs.)

Partition HHH and ggg into removed and kept blocks. For fixed δr\delta_rδr​, the minimizing old variables satisfy

Hmmδm=gm−Hmrδr.H_{mm}\delta_m=g_m-H_{mr}\delta_r.Hmm​δm​=gm​−Hmr​δr​.

If HmmH_{mm}Hmm​ is invertible, substituting back gives a quadratic model on the kept variables alone:

H′=Hrr−HrmHmm−1Hmr,g′=gr−HrmHmm−1gm,c′=c−12gmTHmm−1gm.\begin{aligned} H'&=H_{rr}-H_{rm}H_{mm}^{-1}H_{mr},\\ g'&=g_r-H_{rm}H_{mm}^{-1}g_m,\\ c'&=c-\frac12g_m^TH_{mm}^{-1}g_m. \end{aligned}H′g′c′​=Hrr​−Hrm​Hmm−1​Hmr​,=gr​−Hrm​Hmm−1​gm​,=c−21​gmT​Hmm−1​gm​.​

That’s the Schur complement. (In code, solve the linear system instead of forming the inverse.) The old variables no longer appear in later solves, but they keep influencing the kept ones through H′H'H′, g′g'g′ and the constant. Two current nodes that used to be connected only through an old node can now be directly coupled.

Figure 2: The joint factor after elimination is a compressed form of the original constraints, not a new measurement. Splitting it into two independent priors changes the problem.

📝 Note: In a proper Gaussian model, integrating out the old variables and minimizing over them give the same mean and covariance for the kept variables. The difference is that integration also carries normalization terms like determinants. A profile objective used for MAP isn’t automatically a predictive probability. To compare models, elimination sets or C-segment predictive evidence, you can’t treat the minimized cost as a normalized log-density.

The square-root view: QR on the Jacobian

There’s another route to the same answer that never forms HHH at all. For a linear least-squares problem 12∥Amδm+Arδr−b∥2\frac12\|A_m\delta_m+A_r\delta_r-b\|^221​∥Am​δm​+Ar​δr​−b∥2, orthogonally factor AmA_mAm​. Assuming full column rank, split the full orthogonal matrix as Q=[Q1,Q2]Q=[Q_1,Q_2]Q=[Q1​,Q2​], with Q2Q_2Q2​ spanning the orthogonal complement of AmA_mAm​‘s column space. What’s left after eliminating the old variables is

12∥Bδr−d∥2,B=Q2TAr,d=Q2Tb.\frac12\|B\delta_r-d\|^2, \qquad B=Q_2^TA_r,\quad d=Q_2^Tb.21​∥Bδr​−d∥2,B=Q2T​Ar​,d=Q2T​b.

Then BTB=H′B^TB=H'BTB=H′, BTd=g′B^Td=g'BTd=g′ and 12dTd=c′\frac12d^Td=c'21​dTd=c′. Working at the Jacobian level means the condition number isn’t squared by forming normal equations. Rank-deficient problems still need rank-aware factorizations and an explicit null-space convention; sprinkling jitter on unknown degrees of freedom and calling it the same model doesn’t work.

To check the equivalence numerically, I generated 12 independent random linear systems (seeds 0, 17 and 42 across four old/new dimension pairs). For each, the full least-squares solution, Schur and QR were compared on solution, information matrix, right-hand side, constant and kept covariance, plus the profile objective at five kept-variable values. Every difference was below the pre-set 10−1010^{-10}10−10. (These systems test linear-algebra equivalence only, not route accuracy.)

📝 Further reading: For a systematic treatment of square-root marginalization, see Demmel et al., ICCV 2021, which covers the algebraic relationship between QR and Schur, rank deficiency and floating-point stability. I use it to distinguish “representing the same quadratic problem” from “changing the original problem,” not to transfer its visual-odometry speedups to this navigation system.

The constant term can’t always be dropped either. When minimizing one objective, it doesn’t affect the solution; when comparing objectives across candidates or checking whether history is complete, it’s essential. And if you further compress [B,d][B,d][B,d] into a smaller matrix, the part of ddd orthogonal to BBB‘s column space carries an irreducible residual cost. Compress only BBB and that cost is lost.

When the linearization point is frozen

Everything above is exact for linear problems. Nonlinear factors are linearized at the current state, so the Gaussian prior from marginalization only remembers a local quadratic description at that point. If WiFi later rotates the heading by ten-plus degrees, the old tangent may no longer describe the real motion geometry. Even if every Schur and QR step is perfectly correct, that approximation error remains.

A small controlled SE(2) + Student-t example in the historical records separates the two kinds of difference. Freeze the old Jacobians and Student conditional precisions, then add a new observation that’s still solved nonlinearly. Compare keeping all old frozen factors with eliminating one old published quantity:

  • maximum mean difference: about 1.11×10−151.11\times10^{-15}1.11×10−15;
  • local Laplace covariance difference: about 1.22×10−151.22\times10^{-15}1.22×10−15;
  • profile objective difference (including constants): about 1.17×10−151.17\times10^{-15}1.17×10−15.

So elimination preserves the same frozen approximation. Now relinearize all the old nonlinear factors instead: the route moves by up to about 0.0277 m, and the original objective drops from about 0.39934 to 0.39283. That centimeter-level difference comes from a synthetic toy and isn’t a navigation error bound; it just makes the distinction between frozen history and a full refit concrete. (This round checked the original JSON; the nonlinear solve wasn’t rerun.)

The original receipt also contains two solver_success: false entries. A correlated mixture problem’s gradient was about 2.67×10−92.67\times10^{-9}2.67×10−9. That meets the pre-registered 10−810^{-8}10−8 requirement for the numerical comparison, but not the solver’s requested 10−910^{-9}10−9. Algebraic agreement doesn’t get to rewrite that as “every solve succeeded.”

This has a direct consequence for undoing actions. Retract one action parameter while still using a compressed prior shaped by that action, and the error’s influence may remain. Restore a snapshot from too early without reattaching later legitimate short corrections, and you erase real information. Correct rollback has to say which information is withdrawn, which later events are kept and how common state is re-propagated. Subtracting a translation from trajectory coordinates doesn’t do it.

📝 Further reading: DM-VIO §III-D keeps a delayed graph advanced in the same elimination order as the main graph and uses it to build a new marginalization prior later. Points are still marginalized at their original time and photometric terms are still frozen approximations; it doesn’t “keep all raw history and redo any optimization for free.” Keeping the elimination order matters for bounding the Markov blanket. The lesson I take from it is to give history that may need reinterpretation an explicit scope and replacement mechanism, not to claim the current radio navigation implements DM-VIO.

Nine numbers per node isn’t the whole state

So far we’ve talked about “the state” as if each node’s nine error components (position, velocity, attitude) were all there is. They aren’t. The front end that produces them also carries gyro bias, accelerometer bias and cloned states inside the network window. Nine published dimensions usually aren’t the front end’s full Markov state.

Let the hidden state be ξ\xiξ and two historical outputs eie_iei​ and eje_jej​. They can be correlated through a shared bias or a reused IMU segment. Storing each one’s variance plus neighboring 18×1818\times1818×18 blocks can’t recover all the non-adjacent cross terms. The common covariance post shows how much those correlations affect how position updates reach velocity and attitude.

One bounded representation keeps the joint distribution of the current hidden state, the published states still in use and the action quantities. In a frozen-linearization error process,

ξk+1=Fkξk+Gkwk,\xi_{k+1}=F_k\xi_k+G_kw_k,ξk+1​=Fk​ξk​+Gk​wk​,

and the cross-covariance between old published quantities and the current hidden state propagates too:

Pe,ξk+1=Pe,ξkFkT,P_{e,\xi_{k+1}}=P_{e,\xi_k}F_k^T,Pe,ξk+1​​=Pe,ξk​​FkT​,

assuming the new noise is independent of the kept variables under the model; shared noise needs its cross terms written explicitly. This means reading the propagation, update and clone operations the front end actually executed, not reverse-engineering Fk,GkF_k,G_kFk​,Gk​ from the last two outputs.

Learned measurement updates are especially easy to get wrong. For an update that has already been executed, freezing the actual K,H,RK,H,RK,H,R transforms the estimation error by I−KHI-KHI−KH plus a measurement-noise term. Replaying that error process transports existing uncertainty. Fusing the same network output again as a new independent measurement would count it twice.

Dropping components you no longer need is a submatrix selection in covariance form and a Schur elimination in information form. They describe the same marginal distribution. Seeing a matrix slice in the source code doesn’t mean old information was simply thrown away.

Replaying 700 real propagations

To check this bookkeeping on real data, the historical experiment saved front-end events from a flat-floor walking log (T31), 68.5 to 72.0 s: 700 propagations, 70 learned updates, 70 clone selections and 8 half-second publications. The original publication labels have a registered 5 ms offset from the filter times; I replayed them as recorded rather than quietly fixing the times and reusing old matrices.

The recomputation feeds the saved propagation, update and clone events directly, comparing the full historical matrix with a representation that keeps only the active separator states. The neural network isn’t called again.

They show that two representations of the same frozen error process agree to floating-point precision.

The checker always keeps the first published node and the latest two. As cumulative publications grow, older intermediate nodes are released while the first node’s non-adjacent correlation with new outputs keeps propagating.

Figure 3: Active separator dimension during the replay, against a keep-everything reference.

The front end steadily holds about twenty nine-dimensional clones plus a fifteen-dimensional current state: 20×9+15=19520\times9+15=19520×9+15=195. Keeping three nine-dimensional published quantities on top gives 222. When a new publication arrives before an old node is released, or a new clone is inserted, the transient peak reaches 231. The checker’s capacity guard is 512; 231 is the peak for this stretch of real events and this release policy, not a fixed size for every configuration.

Two cautions about reading this:

  • The 70 internal clone selections and the five historical publication releases are different layers; they shouldn’t both be called “navigation graph marginalization.”
  • This release policy doesn’t show that “keeping three historical nodes is enough at runtime.” If an owner, the map or a candidate still references a fourth node, it has to stay, or be replaced by a statistic proven equivalent.

The replay took about 1.78 s and covers only offline matrix processing of saved events; source capture, front-end inference and navigation solving are excluded, so it’s not a per-window latency. Unmodeled correlations like overlapping network windows aren’t calibrated just because the representations agree numerically.

Interlude: what marginalization keeps, and what it can’t

We can now say precisely what marginalization preserves. For a linear Gaussian problem, it’s the marginal distribution of the kept variables. For a nonlinear system, it’s usually a local approximation around one linearization point. Square-root factorization improves the numerical representation; keeping the common hidden state preserves cross-time dependence. Neither can bring back raw factors that have already been dropped.

So if you’ll ever need to withdraw an old observation or reinterpret history, decide up front which factors, branches or recomputable events to keep. A single reduced prior is enough to continue estimating under the current approximation, but not necessarily enough for those extra jobs.

Part 2: the resource side

The math tells you what to keep. The system has to be able to afford it. Bounded active matrices are only part of the resource story, so the rest of this post looks at three separate things: total allocation, per-window service time and background catch-up. Each has its own inputs and timing scope, and none of them is a performance result of the 700-propagation check above. Per-window data comes from saved evaal_tick_timing.jsonl, recomputed line by line this round. The two solver_success: false entries and the 1 GiB budget receipt are read from historical records, not rerun.

Where 31.3 GiB comes from

At two nine-dimensional nodes per second, keeping the full common covariance for one hour means about 7201 nodes, i.e. a 64809×6480964809\times6480964809×64809 float64 matrix. One copy is about 31.3 GiB, before projection matrices, factorizations, candidate copies and scratch space. Block multiplication can bound the temporary buffers, but not the quadratic growth of the matrix itself.

The converse doesn’t hold either: a fixed number of active nodes doesn’t mean fixed process memory. These have to be accounted for separately:

The old logs contain one concrete capacity bug. A 64 MiB limit meant for a single owner chain was applied to the shared workspace of all candidates. The saved input alone was about 82.12 MiB, and a controlled production-function replay registered a matrix-plus-private-workspace peak bound of about 129.96 MiB, against about 47.99 MiB for the original owner alone. (These are allocation-plan figures, not sampled process peaks.) The system wasn’t out of physical memory; the budget was applied to the wrong object.

A later historical check registered a 1 GiB cap for the common background, with six output identities and the full common-matrix comparison unchanged. That receipt explicitly excludes Python, BLAS, IPC, SQLite and other graph matrices, so 1 GiB is not a process RSS limit. This post reads the saved receipt without rerunning that production function, and doesn’t present “raise the budget” as proof of long-term bounded resources.

A resource budget has to say what the limit applies to, whether it’s checked before or after allocation, whether the old state is preserved on overflow, and what can be released when a consumer finishes. “At most twenty graph nodes” answers none of those.

A FIFO queue on real service times

Averages hide queues, so let’s build one. With a single server processing in order, input gaps ana_nan​ and service times sns_nsn​, waiting satisfies

wn+1=max⁡(0,wn+sn−an),rn=wn+sn.w_{n+1}=\max(0,w_n+s_n-a_n),\qquad r_n=w_n+s_n.wn+1​=max(0,wn​+sn​−an​),rn​=wn​+sn​.

Plug in the historical ENGINE times, assume a window arrives every 0.5 s with no initial backlog, and keep the original order. This is a FIFO scenario computed from real service samples, not a measurement of the historical online queue. It adds no IPC or diagnostic IO, and doesn’t model how service times change under concurrent contention.

Figure 5: Constructed FIFO waiting and response times.

The old baseline’s constructed wait peaks at about 37.62 s, even though its average service time is only 475.09 ms. The new baseline peaks at about 1.14 s and returns to zero. The truncated joint run builds up to about 126.04 s of waiting in the recorded portion. The old-baseline result isn’t paradoxical: idle time early on can’t be banked as capacity for later, so sustained overruns later still pile up.

This figure doesn’t mean “the system measured two minutes of latency.” What it explains is why averages aren’t enough, and it specifies what the next formal replay should measure: true arrival, start, completion and publication times, actual queue depth, and whether the test driver unintentionally throttled input. Stage-level P95s also can’t be summed into a window P95, because whether slow stages land in the same window determines the total distribution.

Can the background candidate catch up?

The historical joint run has a more specific problem than per-window time. At trajectory time 123.5 s, A/B take a snapshot and start computing KEEP, PURE_EN and SE2-related candidates. Meanwhile the foreground keeps consuming Motion, pressure and short corrections. Before handing candidates to C for comparison, they have to splice in all those later inputs, in the same order, up to a common boundary.

In the saved report, the initial construction took about 39.238 s and the first continuation about 62.522 s. At the first READY, about 103.420 s after launch, the foreground had reached about 166 s and the candidates were still behind. After a second continuation of about 15.618 s, the whole job hit its budget and terminated at 120.329 s. That 120 s is a wall-clock compute budget, not the length of a trajectory C segment.

The first coordination phase included about 26.163 s compressing the common response and about 33.312 s advancing the slowest candidate. Three candidates advancing in parallel can’t be summed, and the portion where CPU and wall time are close can’t all be called “waiting for new scans.” These numbers show catch-up requires real matrix work. A fixed cutoff only guarantees each round’s work is finite, not that it finishes in time to catch a foreground that keeps moving.

So three moments must be kept apart: the background finishing the old cutoff; the candidate reaching an input boundary where comparison is fair; and confirmation passing and a correction actually being committed. This run only reached the first. The published trajectory never received this unfinished long correction, so a nice background fit can’t be advertised as improved output.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows that elimination order matters for wrong observations (N(6,1.5) vs. N(1,2)), and that Schur, QR and full least squares agree to 10−1010^{-10}10−10 on 12 random systems.
  • It shows that marginalization preserves the same frozen approximation (differences around 10−1510^{-15}10−15), and that relinearizing changes the answer.
  • It shows that a bounded separator representation reproduces the full matrix on 700 real propagations to about 10−1610^{-16}10−16, with an active dimension peaking at 231.
  • It shows, from real logs, that mean service time hides overruns and that a background candidate fell behind the foreground and hit its budget.
  • It doesn’t include a full-chain replay on a new natural log, a two-arm comparison where a wrong observation enters the actual prior and is then withdrawn, long-term release verification for every candidate/map/rollback consumer, or accuracy, memory and real queue measurements on official long logs.
  • Bounded equivalence for arbitrary relinearization of old nonlinear factors isn’t proven, and the queue curves are a model, not measured latency.

Conclusion

Let’s recap the arc. Marginalization is a Schur complement, or equivalently QR on the Jacobian. It keeps the kept variables’ marginal under the current linearization, and it keeps old mistakes just as faithfully as old truths. Undoing requires history you chose to keep, not a record name. The real state is larger than the nine published numbers, and hidden shared state has to be carried along explicitly. On the resource side, a bounded active window isn’t bounded memory, mean latency isn’t tail latency, and a background candidate that can’t catch up can’t correct anything.

Marginalization doesn’t forget; it compresses, so decide what you’ll need to revisit before you compress it.

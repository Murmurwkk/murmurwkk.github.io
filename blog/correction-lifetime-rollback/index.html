Undoing a Correction Without Losing What Came After

One translation fixed the early trajectory and ruined the late one. How long a navigation correction should live, how to tell it has gone stale, and why undoing it means keeping a fallback that keeps updating.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 10 个章节
#integrated navigation#sequential inference#rollback#posterior#evidence attribution

Your navigation system accepted a long-lived correction. Since then it has kept running: more position observations, more pressure updates, more inertial propagation. Half an hour later you decide the correction was wrong and want to undo it. What do you actually do?

The two obvious answers both lose something:

  • Restore the snapshot from before the correction. That throws away every legitimate update since.
  • Subtract the original correction vector from the current state. That ignores how those later updates interacted with the corrected state through correlation.

What you actually want is a third object: a fallback posterior that has been absorbing every legitimate new observation all along, just without the long-lived model you’re now rejecting. Undoing then means switching branches, not doing arithmetic on the present.

One walking log, which we’ll call the main walk (T33), shows why this isn’t academic. One translation cut the early error by 10 m and then pushed a good late trajectory 8 m off course. In this post we’ll look at that case, pin down what a correction actually claims, and work out the three undo strategies on a small Gaussian example. Then we’ll see how the implementation keeps two branches in sync, what happens when a correction is replaced rather than undone, and why cumulative scores are slow to notice a correction going stale.

The case: a correction that outlived its usefulness

In the main walk, one total translation enters the offline counterfactual scoring from 131.5 s:

teffective=131.5 s,ΔpEN=(−2.705369, 9.976063) m.t_{\mathrm{effective}}=131.5\ \mathrm{s},\qquad \Delta p_{EN}=(-2.705369,\ 9.976063)\ \mathrm{m}.teffective​=131.5 s,ΔpEN​=(−2.705369, 9.976063) m.

“Total” means it replaces the current action’s full amount, rather than being added again on every update. Its recorded expiry is 101210^{12}1012 s, so effectively it never expires within the log.

The action was built from observations; GT/POSI was only used for scoring after the action table was frozen. It was never applied to the production output. The table below is a counterfactual: what the error would be if this fixed translation were applied to the saved trajectory.

Early on it’s a near-perfect fix: 10.308 m down to 0.201 m. By 217 s the baseline had drifted back to within about a meter of the truth on its own, and the still-active translation drags it nearly 10 m away.

That “last quarter” is by path length, not time, and its boundary comes from the original report rather than being chosen after looking at errors.

For contrast, the stairs walk (T25), run in the same period, produced no action at all and stayed at P75/P90 1.625/2.567 m. That’s a useful control: the routing rules don’t always fire. But “doesn’t fire spuriously” and “exits in time when it does fire” are two different requirements, and the main walk fails the second.

What a correction actually claims

Let the baseline position be p(t)p(t)p(t) and the action a fixed translation aaa. Within the local segment where it applies, the claim is

p∗(t)≈p(t)+a.p^{\ast}(t)\approx p(t)+a.p∗(t)≈p(t)+a.

That quietly asserts that one translation explains the segment’s main error. It doesn’t promise the inertial error keeps that direction and size forever, or that later WiFi stays in the same position mode.

If the baseline gradually converges toward the true path, as it did in the main walk, holding the old translation pushes a good trajectory away. Conversely, if the error is direction-dependent, or comes from a floor binding or a wrong map component, just adding an expiry time doesn’t make the model right. A correction’s lifetime should be tied to physical and statistical conditions, not just elapsed seconds.

In code, the action’s state has to cover more than the vector:

ObjectThe question it answers
Model and coordinate identityWhich position hypothesis, which coordinate frame and origin version?
Raw observations usedWhich scans were already used for construction, confirmation or later updates?
Full candidate and fallback state at acceptanceWhich two numerical models were being compared?
Subsequent observation updatesWhat legitimate information was fused after acceptance?
Prediction model for ongoing checksWhat did the model predict before each new observation arrived?
Replacement and exit rulesWhich changes need a new candidate, and which allow undoing?

In the code, an owner represents the current correction model and its full state. Coordinate and source identities decide what gets undone; consumption records decide which observations can still be used afterwards; the candidate and fallback posteriors decide the actual numbers you get when you undo.

Three ways to undo, worked out

In a linear-Gaussian model we can compute exactly how the three undo strategies differ. Take a 2D state (E,U)(E,U)(E,U). The candidate and fallback start at means (10,1)(10,1)(10,1) and (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) with common covariance

P0=(41.21.21).P_0=\begin{pmatrix}4&1.2\\1.2&1\end{pmatrix}.P0​=(41.2​1.21​).

Both branches then receive two independent observations: E=2E=2E=2 with RE=1R_E=1RE​=1, then U=0.4U=0.4U=0.4 with RU=0.25R_U=0.25RU​=0.25. (The U observation just mimics the statistical role of a height update, not a real barometer model.) Each step is standard conditioning:

Because of the correlation, the E observation moves U and the U observation moves E. The gap between the branches, which started at (10,1)(10,1)(10,1), is now about (2.349272,−0.363825)(2.349272,-0.363825)(2.349272,−0.363825). Now compare the three undo strategies:

  • Restore the old snapshot: (0,0)(0,0)(0,0). Both new observations are gone.
  • Subtract the old gap from the candidate: (−6.070686,−0.943035)(-6.070686,-0.943035)(−6.070686,−0.943035). Nowhere near anything sensible.
  • Switch to the live fallback: (1.580042,0.420790)(1.580042,0.420790)(1.580042,0.420790). Both observations kept, old correction removed.

Why did the gap change? In this example both branches have the same covariance, the same linear HHH and the same RRR, so they share the same KKK, and the gap propagates as

Δm+=(I−KH)Δm\Delta m^+=(I-KH)\Delta mΔm+=(I−KH)Δm

The gap gets transformed at every update; it doesn’t stay the vector you started with. With different linearization points, models or covariances, KKK itself differs between branches, and “undo” is even further from a subtraction. And if old nodes have been marginalized, removing old information depends on what joint information was kept when they were (see the sliding-window marginalization post).

How the implementation keeps two branches alive

NativePosteriorFamilyTransaction implements exactly this branch selection. Its prepare_rollback selects the fallback role stored in the current family; it doesn’t reload an old file from acceptance time.

test_native_posterior_payload.py runs 12 test cases, all passing. The full-sequence case uses two separate local posterior views. It commits a pressure reference, then does an EN update, native propagation with old-node release, the next pressure update, a rollback, and finally one more fresh EN. The global count is this fixture’s EN consumption ledger, not an arrival count for all sensors. Pressure has its own index, so “the count didn’t increase” doesn’t mean pressure wasn’t updated.

A few details show the semantics are right:

  • Each branch keeps a 36-dimensional matrix. After the first EN, the covariance trace drops from 36.54 to about 32.76047, and that information survives the old target’s release.
  • Rollback leaves the fallback’s numerical bridge identity unchanged, keeps the pressure index at 1 and doesn’t disturb existing C scoring contexts.
  • Updates are transactional. They’re prepared on each separate full view, and only if every branch succeeds are the global consumption records written and the new family adopted. Tests cover stale contexts, tampered pending objects, one branch failing to prepare and an observation already consumed elsewhere. You can’t update the branch that succeeded and leave the other stuck in the past.
  • Consuming the same raw EN twice is rejected. A cross-branch covariance query is also rejected when the branches are separate local noise coordinates with no common-source representation; keeping two candidates doesn’t mean their joint noise model is built correctly.

Replacing a correction: when the reference component changes

Undoing isn’t the only way a correction ends; it can also be replaced by a successor. The main walk has one reference-component change that ties together replacement, source consumption and the budget for repeated attempts.

The old A/B at 171.5/174.0 s froze one reference component. At 195.5 s, an observation from a different component arrived. The corrected rule first retires the old candidate with NONE, without letting it consume the incompatible new data. Only then can that still-unclaimed 195.5 s observation become the new A, with 198.0 s as the new B.

The new candidate gets

anew=(−0.216514, 3.500225) m,R=0.890372I m2.a_{\mathrm{new}}=(-0.216514,\ 3.500225)\ \mathrm m, \qquad R=0.890372I\ \mathrm m^2.anew​=(−0.216514, 3.500225) m,R=0.890372I m2.

Two new C observations at 200.0 and 202.5 s give a cumulative PURE-vs-NULL score difference of 9.106831. The bound for this run’s 6th candidate chain is 9.274176, a shortfall of 0.167345. So it stays DEFER, and later retires when yet another component appears at 210.0 s.

Here’s the temptation: reset the candidate index to 2 and the bound drops to 7.076952, which this score would clear easily. But that changes how the whole run spends its risk budget for repeated attempts. Fixing the data routing doesn’t erase the attempts already made, and a new candidate that looks useful doesn’t earn a looser budget.

The same logic applies to roles. A scan that served as some candidate’s C can’t later be repackaged as another candidate’s A/B. It already took part in a decision, and renaming its role doesn’t make it independent again. (The division of construction, confirmation and availability is covered in A/B/C and frozen prediction.)

The saved structural checks show the main walk’s 55 observation blocks and 1,521 raw events each completed exactly one terminal attribution, with 5 component-change reprocessings; the stairs walk has 16 blocks, 725 events and 2 reprocessings. That supports that this version’s routing structure is fixed. It doesn’t show its likelihood was false-alarm calibrated on natural logs, and it certainly doesn’t show the late-segment damage was undone. The main-walk error table above is exactly why those two claims must be kept apart.

When to undo: cumulative scores remember old wins

Knowing how to undo is half the problem; the other half is noticing when. The common approach is to keep accumulating the predictive log-likelihood ratio between the fallback and the current action after acceptance:

Ln=∑i=1nlog⁡p(zi∣Mrollback,Fi−1)p(zi∣Mkeep,Fi−1).L_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log\frac{p(z_i\mid M_{\mathrm{rollback}},\mathcal F_{i-1})} {p(z_i\mid M_{\mathrm{keep}},\mathcal F_{i-1})}.Ln​=i=1∑n​logp(zi​∣Mkeep​,Fi−1​)p(zi​∣Mrollback​,Fi−1​)​.

Undo once LnL_nLn​ is large enough. The problem is memory. If the action was genuinely great early on, the sum has banked a large negative balance. When it goes bad later, the monitor first has to pay off that “old advantage.” This statistic is really answering “which fixed model has done better since the beginning?”, not “has the current model just stopped working?”

An older three-model historical report actually recorded this. KEEP-minus-PURE cumulative log score was about −900 before the late change and still about −707.486 at the end, never crossing its upper bound of 3.483382. Those numbers are from an older report, not a rerun of the main walk’s total translation above, and the old action vector was slightly different. Its full step-by-step inputs weren’t recovered, so that monitor can’t be rerun.

To isolate the mechanism, here’s a small, reproducible constructed example. The first 225 observations are drawn from N(0,1)\mathcal N(0,1)N(0,1) and the next 100 from N(2,1)\mathcal N(2,1)N(2,1), with fixed seed 17. Comparing two pre-fixed distributions, the per-step log likelihood ratio is

ℓt=log⁡ϕ(yt−2)ϕ(yt)=2yt−2.\ell_t=\log\frac{\phi(y_t-2)}{\phi(y_t)}=2y_t-2.ℓt​=logϕ(yt​)ϕ(yt​−2)​=2yt​−2.

Accumulating from the first sample, the expected step is −2 before the change and +2 after. With a finite post-change segment, the running sum may never get back above zero.

Resetting looks fastest, but you can’t apply a single test’s error probability to it: every new starting point is another chance for the algorithm to fire. In this known-distribution example, you can pre-assign weights to the start at τ\tauτ,

A new start doesn’t have to repay all the old negative score, but it carries its own small weight, so the delay isn’t free.

Before plugging anything like this into navigation, you’d need to show the observation definition, conditional predictions, data correlations and candidate selection all satisfy the corresponding assumptions. Passing old scores into a function named e-process doesn’t buy a false-alarm guarantee.

How fair is the fallback comparison?

With a live fallback branch, it’s tempting to read the difference between branches as “the effect of the correction.” NativeOwnerEffectRecorder does record that difference, after checking that both branches refer to the same physical time, target, coordinate frame and gauge epoch. But the source code is careful to call this “the actual fallback policy that keeps later local updates,” and notes it’s not an unconditional causal effect.

The reason: the set of observations the counterfactual fallback absorbed may itself have been shaped by decisions in the actual run. Under a different long-lived model, later candidates, observation routing and map choices might all have changed. So the two-branch comparison answers how the two state interpretations differ, given the update policy and data history that actually happened. It can’t say what a different complete system would have produced from scratch.

To claim that a rollback strategy improves navigation, you’d need a comparable run protocol: save actual availability times, freeze candidates and undo rules, and compare complete continuous runs at the same reference times. Record whether and when actions executed, whether the fallback stayed current and how many replacements occurred, rather than reporting the error right after one successful switch.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows that in the main walk one fixed translation improved early reference errors by up to 10.107 m and degraded late ones by up to 8.737 m, and that the stairs-walk control produced no action.
  • It shows, exactly, that after correlated updates neither restoring a snapshot nor subtracting the old gap equals the live fallback posterior.
  • It shows that the current component’s transactional semantics are correct on constructed inputs (12 passing tests), and that the routing structure on both walks gives every event exactly one terminal attribution.
  • It doesn’t show the full accept → update → degrade → undo chain on a natural log; no action was executed on production output, and there’s no natural replay.
  • It doesn’t cover crash recovery, runtime resources or online latency.
  • The cumulative-score memory effect is shown on a constructed sequence; the old monitor used on the main walk can’t be rerun, and no false-alarm rate is measured.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. A correction makes a claim, that one transform explains the current error, and that claim has a lifetime that should be tied to conditions, not a clock. Noticing that it’s gone stale is a monitoring problem, and cumulative scores are slow at it because they remember old wins. Undoing it is a state problem. Once later observations have been fused, the right undo is to switch to a fallback posterior that kept absorbing them, not to restore or subtract. Replacing a correction has its own rules: retire the old candidate before the new data, and don’t reset the risk budget.

Monitoring and state are separate requirements, and both have to hold. When this chain is run end to end on a natural log, it should record action lifetimes, replacement times, monitoring delay and every scorable reference point, with no-action controls like the stairs walk alongside.

Undoing a correction isn’t going back in time; it’s choosing the version of the present that never believed it.

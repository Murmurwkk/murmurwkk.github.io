How a First-Order Rotation Stretched an 18 m Walk to 25 m

A correction cut reference error from 24.5 m to 4.8 m and made the route 40% longer. Why a large first-order rotation also scales, checked on all 146 trajectory edges, and how relinearizing fixes it.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 10 个章节
#SE(2)#factor graphs#linearization#case study#reproducible experiments

Here’s a correction from a flat-floor walking log (T31) that looks like a clear win. The mean horizontal error at four sparse reference points dropped from 24.51 m to 4.75 m. Ship it?

Look at the shape of the trajectory, though, and something’s off. A displacement that measured 18.07 m before the correction measures 25.14 m after it. The corrected route is closer to the reference points and about 40% longer than the walk it’s supposed to describe.

Both statements are true, because they measure different things. Distance to reference points says whether the trajectory landed in the right place. The motion between neighboring states says whether it’s still a plausible walk. This correction passed the first test and failed the second, and the reason turns out to be one line of linear algebra: position was updated with I+θJI+\theta JI+θJ, attitude with a true rotation, and at nearly one radian those two no longer describe the same motion.

In this post we’ll start with the geometry of a first-order rotation, since that already predicts the stretch. Then we’ll look at that walk, confirm the prediction on every trajectory edge, see how a “position-only” update ends up rotating attitude, and end with the fix and its results.

Fundamentals: a rotation and its first-order impostor

Take an edge of length 10 pointing along the x-axis, (10,0)(10,0)(10,0), and rotate it by 1 rad.

  • A true rotation gives about (5.403,8.415)(5.403,8.415)(5.403,8.415). Length: still 10.
  • The first-order update gives (10,10)(10,10)(10,10). Length: 10210\sqrt2102​. Direction: only 45°.

The first-order point lands on the tangent line of the circle, not on the circle. For tiny steps the difference is negligible. Use it as one big final update, and both length and direction come out wrong.

Figure 1: Tangent vs. circle, drawn with case A’s perturbation angle and starting length. Pure-yaw approximation only; the tangent length isn’t a real trajectory end-point.

Let’s make that precise. A 2D rotation is

R(θ)=[cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ],R⊤R=I,R(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad R^\top R=I,R(θ)=[cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​],R⊤R=I,

so ∥R(θ)d∥=∥d∥\|R(\theta)d\|=\|d\|∥R(θ)d∥=∥d∥ for every ddd. Rigid motions turn and translate; they can’t make a corridor bigger. The first-order expansion around zero is

R(θ)≈I+θJ,J=[0−110].R(\theta)\approx I+\theta J,\qquad J=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.R(θ)≈I+θJ,J=[01​−10​].

Since J⊤=−JJ^\top=-JJ⊤=−J and J⊤J=IJ^\top J=IJ⊤J=I,

(I+θJ)⊤(I+θJ)=(1+θ2)I.(I+\theta J)^\top(I+\theta J) =(1+\theta^2)I.(I+θJ)⊤(I+θJ)=(1+θ2)I.

That gives a clean factorization of what the approximation actually does when you apply it as a finite transform:

∥(I+θJ)d∥=1+θ2 ∥d∥,I+θJ=1+θ2 R(arctan⁡θ).\|(I+\theta J)d\| =\sqrt{1+\theta^2}\,\|d\|, \qquad I+\theta J=\sqrt{1+\theta^2}\,R(\arctan\theta).∥(I+θJ)d∥=1+θ2​∥d∥,I+θJ=1+θ2​R(arctanθ).

So I+θJI+\theta JI+θJ is a rotation by arctan⁡θ\arctan\thetaarctanθ followed by a uniform scale of 1+θ2\sqrt{1+\theta^2}1+θ2​. The rotation is too small and the scale is greater than one. For the perturbation in this case, θ=0.946055\theta=0.946055θ=0.946055 rad, that’s a length multiplier of 1.376597 and a rotation of 43.412°. Keep those two numbers in mind; we’re going to find them in the data.

In 3D, with Ω=[ω]×\Omega=[\omega]_\timesΩ=[ω]×​, the same calculation gives

(I+Ω)⊤(I+Ω)=I−Ω2=(1+∥ω∥2)I−ωω⊤.(I+\Omega)^\top(I+\Omega) =I-\Omega^2 =(1+\|\omega\|^2)I-\omega\omega^\top.(I+Ω)⊤(I+Ω)=I−Ω2=(1+∥ω∥2)I−ωω⊤.

The component along the rotation axis isn’t stretched; perpendicular components are multiplied by 1+∥ω∥2\sqrt{1+\|\omega\|^2}1+∥ω∥2​. When the rotation is mostly about the vertical axis, as it is for a walking pedestrian’s yaw, the 2D picture is exactly what you see in the horizontal plane.

None of this is a flaw in Gauss–Newton, LM or Kalman filters. They all run on local approximations, and local approximations are fine locally. The failure mode is specific: after one large step, attitude has been moved to a new linearization point while position is still sitting on the old tangent.

The case: a correction that overshot in length

Case A from the flat-floor walk stores 147 states, 146 neighboring edges and 6 representative radio observations. Four POSI reference points are used purely for evaluation; two of them are at the same location, so they overlap on the plot.

Testing the hypothesis, edge by edge

End-point distances tell us the trajectory deformed. To test whether a first-order rotation explains this particular deformation, compare every neighboring displacement before and after the correction:

di=pi+1−pi,di∗=pi+1∗−pi∗.d_i=p_{i+1}-p_i,\qquad d_i^*=p_{i+1}^*-p_i^*.di​=pi+1​−pi​,di∗​=pi+1∗​−pi∗​.

The common translation EaEaEa cancels in the difference, so fitting di,EN∗≈Mdi,ENd_{i,EN}^*\approx M d_{i,EN}di,EN∗​≈Mdi,EN​ reveals the actual linear shape of the position update, unaffected by the internal translation parameter. For case A’s saved coordinates:

M=[1.002909−0.9527230.9398960.996521].M= \begin{bmatrix} 1.002909&-0.952723\\ 0.939896&0.996521 \end{bmatrix}.M=[1.0029090.939896​−0.9527230.996521​].

Its singular values tell us how much it stretches:

M=UΣV⊤,Σ=diag⁡(1.383731,  1.369400).M=U\Sigma V^\top,\qquad \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(1.383731,\;1.369400).M=UΣV⊤,Σ=diag(1.383731,1.369400).

Both directions are stretched by about 1.37–1.38, right where the theory said to look. The rotation part of the polar decomposition, UV⊤UV^\topUV⊤, comes out at 43.428°, against 43.412° predicted from the world rotation increment. And this single 2D map fits all 146 edges with a vector RMS residual of about 7.003 mm.

The saved per-state 3D computation makes the same point from a different angle. Predict each neighboring edge from that state’s own world rotation increment:

  • with the first-order model, the RMS is about 0.006821 m;
  • with the same increment applied as a finite Exp, the RMS for predicting these old position edges is about 0.213481 m, roughly 31 times worse.

Read that carefully: it doesn’t say finite rotations are less correct. It says the old position results were produced by a first-order map, so they’re incompatible with an attitude that already received a finite update. Position and attitude are now telling two different stories.

The 2D regression, SVD, polar decomposition and end-point lengths were all recomputed from saved coordinates (the 3D residuals are from the original computation record). The core is a handful of lines:

before_edges = np.diff(before_positions[:, :2], axis=0)
after_edges = np.diff(linear_positions[:, :2], axis=0)
# One row per edge; transpose to get M in the column-vector convention.
row_map, _, rank, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(before_edges, after_edges, rcond=None)
M = row_map.T
U, scales, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
R_polar = U @ Vt
angle = np.arctan2(R_polar[1, 0], R_polar[0, 0])

The real code also checks that the 2D design matrix has full rank and compares against the sealed matrix. On a perfectly straight trajectory, the 2D map wouldn’t be identifiable, so not every least-squares MMM deserves to be called “the observed deformation.”

How a position-only update ends up rotating attitude

There’s a puzzle here. The old update path was called PURE_EN, because the radio factor only observes horizontal position:

zi=pi,EN+ϵi.z_i=p_{i,EN}+\epsilon_i.zi​=pi,EN​+ϵi​.

The attitude Jacobian of that factor is zero. So where did a 43° attitude change come from?

From the rest of the graph. The state carries a joint prior over position, velocity and attitude, and Motion factors tie neighboring states together. A position observation propagates through those correlations into everything else. The old conditioning problem also had a common horizontal translation aaa with no independent prior. With local perturbation δx\delta xδx, common covariance PPP, position selector HHH, translation replicator AAA and radio residual y=z−Hxy=z-Hxy=z−Hx, the linear-Gaussian objective is

min⁡δx,a  12δx⊤P−1δx+12(y−Hδx−Aa)⊤R−1(y−Hδx−Aa).\min_{\delta x,a}\; \frac12\delta x^\top P^{-1}\delta x+ \frac12(y-H\delta x-Aa)^\top R^{-1} (y-H\delta x-Aa).δx,amin​21​δx⊤P−1δx+21​(y−Hδx−Aa)⊤R−1(y−Hδx−Aa).

Eliminating δx\delta xδx:

S=HPH⊤+R,S=HPH^\top+R,S=HPH⊤+R, a=(A⊤S−1A)−1A⊤S−1y,δx=PH⊤S−1(y−Aa).a=(A^\top S^{-1}A)^{-1}A^\top S^{-1}y, \qquad \delta x=PH^\top S^{-1}(y-Aa).a=(A⊤S−1A)−1A⊤S−1y,δx=PH⊤S−1(y−Aa).

(The implementation solves these with factorizations rather than explicit inverses.) Everything interesting is in PH⊤PH^\topPH⊤. The observation selects only EN, but PH⊤PH^\topPH⊤ is nonzero in the velocity and attitude rows. “There’s no yaw parameter in this update” and “yaw doesn’t change in this update” are two different statements, and only the first one was true.

The physical states are then formed as

pi∗=pi+δpi+Ea,vi∗=vi+δvi,Ri∗=RiExp⁡([δθi]×),p_i^*=p_i+\delta p_i+Ea,\qquad v_i^*=v_i+\delta v_i,\qquad R_i^*=R_i\operatorname{Exp}([\delta\theta_i]_\times),pi∗​=pi​+δpi​+Ea,vi∗​=vi​+δvi​,Ri∗​=Ri​Exp([δθi​]×​),

where EEE embeds the 2D translation in 3D. And there it is: position gets the additive, first-order δpi\delta p_iδpi​, while attitude gets a finite Exp⁡\operatorname{Exp}Exp. The attitude δθi\delta\theta_iδθi​ is a right-side local perturbation, so comparing it with world-frame edges requires converting it first.

📝 Further reading: For left vs. right perturbations, the exponential map and their Jacobians, Solà et al.’s micro Lie theory tutorial is the standard reference.1

Was the linear problem at least solved correctly? Yes: an independent historical QR solution matches the original GLS translation to about 1.14×10−131.14\times10^{-13}1.14×10−13 m for case A. The linear algebra is fine; the problem is that after a large step, it’s no longer the right geometric problem.

One more diagnostic trap: the internal translation aaa can reach tens of meters here, and δpi\delta p_iδpi​ partly cancels it. Looking at ∥a∥\|a\|∥a∥ alone tells you neither the final displacement nor whether attitude and position agree. You have to look at the full pi∗p_i^*pi∗​ and its edges.

Fixing it properly

The geometry has to go back to where the state correlations came from: the Motion factors. The position and velocity residuals between neighbors are

rp,ij=Ri⊤(pj−pi)−Δpij,rv,ij=Ri⊤(vj−vi)−Δvij,r_{p,ij}=R_i^\top(p_j-p_i)-\Delta p_{ij}, \qquad r_{v,ij}=R_i^\top(v_j-v_i)-\Delta v_{ij},rp,ij​=Ri⊤​(pj​−pi​)−Δpij​,rv,ij​=Ri⊤​(vj​−vi​)−Δvij​,

with the attitude residual built from the Lie-group log of the measured relative rotation against Ri⊤RjR_i^\top R_jRi⊤​Rj​. Together they say: if attitude changes, position and velocity must re-satisfy the relative motion. Keeping the quaternion normalized doesn’t count as fixing the geometry.

The offline nonlinear program restored a boundary prior, the original Motion factors in the window and the selected Radio observations. Each iteration evaluated true residuals and Jacobians at the current state, applied the increment with a finite rotation, and accepted or rejected the step against the recomputed objective. Damping controls the solver step; it’s not a physical rule like “correct by at most N meters.”

Two details are easy to get wrong:

  • Don’t double count. The full joint PPP already contains the Motion information, so you can’t use it as an extra prior and add the original Motion factors. Rebuilding the nonlinear problem requires knowing how the boundary prior and internal factors decompose; the final marginal covariance alone isn’t always enough.
  • A small step isn’t convergence. Heavy damping shrinks steps while the gradient is still large. A small step can be a stopping reason; it isn’t automatically convergence. The historical solver records objective, gradient and stopping reason, and all four results below are explicitly saved original-weight solutions.

Can the same radio data explain an unstretched route?

Before the full nonlinear solve, the historical study ran a simpler sanity check. Keep the route’s internal shape, use the same representative radio groups and original QQQ, and fit only a finite rotation and translation:

min⁡θ,t∑i(R(θ)pi+t−zi)⊤Qi−1(R(θ)pi+t−zi).\min_{\theta,t} \sum_i \bigl(R(\theta)p_i+t-z_i\bigr)^\top Q_i^{-1} \bigl(R(\theta)p_i+t-z_i\bigr).θ,tmin​i∑​(R(θ)pi​+t−zi​)⊤Qi−1​(R(θ)pi​+t−zi​).

No free scale, no POSI in the objective. For fixed θ\thetaθ the translation is linear; θ\thetaθ can be searched over the full circle and cross-checked at stationary points. The question is simply whether the radio evidence requires a stretched route. It doesn’t: an unstretched rigid fit is compatible with it. This rigid fit treats the trajectory shape as known and drops the state’s common uncertainty, though, so it’s a sanity check, not a navigation solution. The green curve in Figure 2 is the nonlinear Motion solution, not this rigid fit.

Why not just scale the output back?

The tempting shortcut is: we know it stretched by about 1.38, so divide. That doesn’t work, for several reasons:

  • The scale comes from one snapshot’s rotation increment; another snapshot has different state changes, observation support and common prior.
  • In 3D the stretch depends on direction, so there’s no single number to divide by.
  • Rescaling output breaks the relationships between position, velocity, attitude and the boundary prior. It doesn’t restore the Motion residuals and it doesn’t produce a correct covariance.

Clamping the internal translation doesn’t help either. It might mask the distortion for a while, and it would also block genuine large drift from being corrected. The fix belongs in the problem’s geometry and solver, not in a clamp tuned to one case.

This resolves the opening contradiction. The old linear solution really did get closer to the references. Same-weight relinearization gets closer still, and restores the main length relationships, so the error reduction no longer comes bundled with a stretched route.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows that the stretch in case A is quantitatively explained by applying a first-order rotation as a finite update: predicted scale 1.376597, measured 1.37–1.38; predicted angle 43.412°, measured 43.428°.
  • It shows that same-weight relinearization with finite rotations reduces reference error further while restoring end-point lengths.
  • The return leg is still slightly short, and B and C have one reference point each, so this doesn’t establish that the whole route is accurate.
  • These are offline smoothed solutions, where later observations revise earlier states. They don’t show that the real-time output had this accuracy, and they don’t test pause velocity, cross-time correlation or cross-device generalization.
  • The four snapshots overlap and come from one log; they’re not independent trials.

Conclusion

Here’s the story in one paragraph. A first-order rotation used as a large update is a smaller rotation plus a scale of 1+θ2\sqrt{1+\theta^2}1+θ2​. At almost one radian that’s a 38% stretch, and that’s what all 146 edges of case A show. A “position-only” radio factor still reaches attitude through the joint prior and the Motion factors, so attitude got a finite rotation while position stayed on the tangent. Relinearizing the original nonlinear problem fixes it; scaling or clamping the output doesn’t.

The checking habit this leaves me with is simple. After accepting any large local increment, recompute the original nonlinear residuals and check that finite attitude still agrees with the relative motion of position and velocity. A small linear-system residual only says this round’s linear equations were solved correctly. The edge-by-edge map is what tells you what the update actually did to the trajectory.

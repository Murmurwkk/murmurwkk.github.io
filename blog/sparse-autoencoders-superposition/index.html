When a Sparse Autoencoder Reconstructs Better and Finds Less

On data with 12 known directions, letting each sample use 4 latents instead of 2 lowered an SAE's reconstruction error and halved the directions it recovered. Reconstruction isn't explanation.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 7 个章节
#sparse autoencoders#interpretability#Superposition#TopK#paper deep dive

Here’s an experiment with a known answer. Take 12 known directions, mix them sparsely into an 8-dimensional signal, and hand it to a sparse autoencoder (SAE) with a dictionary of width 24. Can it find the 12 directions again?

Across three runs each, the answer depends on one knob in an uncomfortable way. When each sample may use at most 2 latents, all 12 directions come back, every time. Loosen that to 4, and reconstruction error gets lower, while the share of recovered directions drops to about half.

Reconstruction got better. Recovery of the directions that actually generated the data got worse. We had the answer in hand, and the model chose a representation that reconstructs better instead.

That’s not a bug in the training; it’s what the objective says. An SAE is trained to do two things: reconstruct the original activation, and keep its representation sparse. “Recover the features the model actually uses” is what interpretability wants, and it isn’t in the objective. And the same activation can often be assembled in more than one equivalent way, which you can already see in one dimension.

In this post we’ll start with how two features can share one coordinate, and why that leaves the decomposition ambiguous. Then we’ll build up the SAE itself, see how L1, TopK and JumpReLU each enforce sparsity, run 27 trainings on data with a known answer, and finish with what it takes to go from “reconstructs well” to “explains the model.”

Fundamentals: two features in one coordinate

Take two non-negative features f1,f2f_1,f_2f1​,f2​ and keep only their difference:

h=f1−f2.h=f_1-f_2.h=f1​−f2​.

As long as they never appear together, both can be recovered exactly:

f^1=ReLU⁡(h),f^2=ReLU⁡(−h).\hat f_1=\operatorname{ReLU}(h),\qquad \hat f_2=\operatorname{ReLU}(-h).f^​1​=ReLU(h),f^​2​=ReLU(−h).

The sign separates them and the magnitude stores the strength. No extra dimension is used; what makes it work is a restriction on the input distribution: allowed states lie only on the two non-negative axes, not across the whole plane.

Break the restriction and information is lost. (f1,f2)=(0.8,0.3)(f_1,f_2)=(0.8,0.3)(f1​,f2​)=(0.8,0.3) and (0.5,0)(0.5,0)(0.5,0) both give h=0.5h=0.5h=0.5, and the decoder can only return (0.5,0)(0.5,0)(0.5,0). When both are positive, the summed squared error is 2min⁡(f1,f2)22\min(f_1,f_2)^22min(f1​,f2​)2. But if each feature independently appears with probability ppp, such collisions only happen with probability p2p^2p2. When features are sparse enough, an occasional collision is cheaper than permanently dropping one of them.

Toy Models of Superposition turned this trade-off into a training experiment. Inputs are known sparse features, the model compresses them into a low-dimensional space and recovers them, and feature importance and frequency change which directions get packed together. Superposition means there are more usable features than representation dimensions, with several features sharing space along non-orthogonal directions.

Generalizing the 1D sign trick to ddd dimensions:

h=D∗f=∑j=1mdjfj,D∗∈Rd×m,m>d.h=D_*f=\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_jf_j,\qquad D_*\in\mathbb R^{d\times m},\quad m>d.h=D∗​f=j=1∑m​dj​fj​,D∗​∈Rd×m,m>d.

Each column of D∗D_*D∗​ is a direction and fjf_jfj​ its non-negative strength. A single neuron records

hi=∑j(D∗)ijfj.h_i=\sum_j(D_*)_{ij}f_j.hi​=j∑​(D∗​)ij​fj​.

So one coordinate responds to many features. Looking up a neuron’s most activating inputs may not pin down a unique meaning, because it may be the projection of several directions onto that coordinate.

This model is deliberately simplified. It doesn’t say every concept in a real language model is its own dimension, and it doesn’t say network coordinates can be rotated freely without changing behavior; around coordinate-wise nonlinearities, the original basis still takes part in the computation. The SAE’s assumption is narrower: at one chosen activation site, can a set of sparsely active directions approximate it well enough?

Sparsity doesn’t make the answer unique

Even sparsity isn’t enough to make the decomposition unique, as a 2D example shows. Put five unit directions evenly around a circle, 72° apart. Then

d0+d2=2cos⁡(72∘)d1≈0.618034d1.d_0+d_2=2\cos(72^\circ)d_1\approx0.618034d_1.d0​+d2​=2cos(72∘)d1​≈0.618034d1​.

If the generating process really used d0+d2d_0+d_2d0​+d2​, an algorithm that prefers fewer nonzeros will pick the other explanation. Resolving some of that ambiguity takes more samples, using statistics like which directions often appear alone and which combinations are rare. A distinction that isn’t in the data can’t be supplied by a reconstruction objective.

The SAE: a dictionary and a detector are two different tables

Now suppose hhh comes from a trained language model. We can collect lots of hhh, but we can’t see the generating dictionary D∗D_*D∗​ or coefficients fff. An SAE learns its own dictionary DDD and trains an encoder to guess coefficients from hhh:

u=Wenc(h−b)+benc,z=ReLU⁡(u),h^=Dz+b.u=W_{\mathrm{enc}}(h-b)+b_{\mathrm{enc}},\qquad z=\operatorname{ReLU}(u),\qquad \hat h=Dz+b.u=Wenc​(h−b)+benc​,z=ReLU(u),h^=Dz+b.

Here h,h^,b∈Rdh,\hat h,b\in\mathbb R^dh,h^,b∈Rd and z∈Rmz\in\mathbb R^mz∈Rm, usually with m>dm>dm>d. A decoder column says “when this latent turns on, the activation moves this way.” An encoder row says “this is the input that turns it on.” Those are two different tables.

For example, use the dictionary’s transpose directly as the encoder:

D⊤h=D⊤D∗f.D^\top h=D^\top D_*f.D⊤h=D⊤D∗​f.

Even if we happened to learn D=D∗D=D_*D=D∗​ exactly, non-orthogonal directions have nonzero inner products, so other features leak into each detector. Initializing the encoder as the decoder’s transpose is common, but keeping them tied is an extra constraint, not something that follows from “encoder and decoder should be inverses.”

A plain ReLU SAE minimizes

L=Eh[12∥h−h^∥22+λ∥z∥1].\mathcal L= \mathbb E_h\left[ \frac12\|h-\hat h\|_2^2+\lambda\|z\|_1 \right].L=Eh​[21​∥h−h^∥22​+λ∥z∥1​].

The first term asks Dz+bDz+bDz+b to match the activation; the second penalizes the total magnitude of the code. Without the second, a wide enough autoencoder can just learn to copy its input, with no reason to separate factors that often co-occur. The sparsity penalty makes how it reconstructs part of the requirement.

There’s a loophole. Scale column djd_jdj​ up by ccc and zjz_jzj​ down by ccc, and reconstruction is unchanged while L1 gets smaller. In practice you constrain decoder column norms, or use a scale-invariant penalty like ∑j∣zj∣∥dj∥2\sum_j|z_j|\|d_j\|_2∑j​∣zj​∣∥dj​∥2​. The experiment below keeps every column at unit norm. That fixes scale; permutation, duplicate directions and the ambiguity from the previous section all remain.

Towards Monosemanticity studied such dictionaries on the 512-dimensional MLP activations of a one-layer Transformer, with 4096 latents in the version analyzed in detail. 4096 is larger than 512, but what grows is the number of candidate directions, not the number used each time. Dictionary width and how many latents may be active have to be controlled separately; otherwise, when error drops, you can’t tell whether it’s because there are more options or because each sample turned on more directions.

Why L1 shrinks coefficients

Keep a single unit direction and remove its contribution to reconstruction. Say the remaining positive input projection is ttt and the coefficient is a≥0a\ge0a≥0. The L1 subproblem has a closed form:

min⁡a≥012(t−a)2+λ1a⟹a∗=max⁡(t−λ1,0).\min_{a\ge0}\frac12(t-a)^2+\lambda_1a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^*=\max(t-\lambda_1,0).a≥0min​21​(t−a)2+λ1​a⟹a∗=max(t−λ1​,0).

So L1 doesn’t just zero out small values. Even a coefficient large enough to be clearly on gets λ1\lambda_1λ1​ subtracted. Paying per unit of magnitude shrinks everything.

If instead you charge only for turning on, i.e. an L0 count:

min⁡a≥012(t−a)2+λ01[a>0],\min_{a\ge0}\frac12(t-a)^2+\lambda_0\mathbf1[a>0],a≥0min​21​(t−a)2+λ0​1[a>0],

then once it’s on, the best magnitude is ttt. You just compare the two choices: off costs t2/2t^2/2t2/2, on costs λ0\lambda_0λ0​.

a∗={t,t>2λ0,0,t<2λ0.a^*= \begin{cases} t,&t>\sqrt{2\lambda_0},\\ 0,&t<\sqrt{2\lambda_0}. \end{cases}a∗={t,0,​t>2λ0​​,t<2λ0​​.​

At equality the two tie. This scalar derivation can’t solve for all coefficients under a non-orthogonal dictionary, but it pinpoints the difference between the two kinds of objective: you can limit how many terms are active without also penalizing the size of the active ones.

TopK and JumpReLU: two ways to cap activity

TopK gives each sample a fixed budget: keep only the kkk largest encoder pre-activations. Gao et al. study scaling laws for this kind of SAE, evaluating reconstruction, interpretability and downstream effect separately. The core of the public implementation is a few lines:

topk = torch.topk(x, k=self.k, dim=-1)
values = self.postact_fn(topk.values)
result = torch.zeros_like(x)
result.scatter_(-1, topk.indices, values)
return result

Here xxx is the encoder pre-activation, not the language model’s activation, and the last dimension runs over every latent in the dictionary, so competition happens within each sample. The code is from OpenAI sparse_autoencoder/model.py at a pinned commit, under the MIT LICENSE.

One ordering detail affects interpretation: the default ReLU runs after selecting the top values. Feed [−5,−3,−1,−7][-5,-3,-1,-7][−5,−3,−1,−7] into the real implementation with k=2k=2k=2, and the result is all zeros. So TopK guarantees at most kkk nonzeros. Selected pre-activations no longer have an L1 threshold subtracted, but they’re still the encoder’s estimates, not true feature strengths.

TopK’s threshold moves with each sample. A direction’s pre-activation can be unchanged and still drop out of the top kkk because other directions in the same sample are stronger. That’s good for hard compute or storage budgets, but it doesn’t necessarily match input complexity: a simple input and one with many factors mixed together get the same number of slots.

JumpReLU puts the threshold back on each direction:

zj=uj1[uj>θj],L=∥h−h^∥22+λ∥z∥0.z_j=u_j\mathbf1[u_j>\theta_j],\qquad \mathcal L=\|h-\hat h\|_2^2+\lambda\|z\|_0.zj​=uj​1[uj​>θj​],L=∥h−h^∥22​+λ∥z∥0​.

Pass the threshold and the full magnitude is kept; miss it and the latent is off. Different inputs can turn on different numbers of directions, and the thresholds θj\theta_jθj​ are learned. The catch: for a fixed sample, the output doesn’t change unless the threshold crosses uju_juj​, so ordinary autodiff gives no threshold gradient almost everywhere.

The output of one sample doesn’t change, but the average loss over the input distribution can. Back to the scalar problem: let t∼U[0,2]t\sim U[0,2]t∼U[0,2] and output a=t1[t>θ]a=t\mathbf1[t>\theta]a=t1[t>θ]. For 0≤θ≤20\le\theta\le20≤θ≤2, the loss from switched-off samples is ∫0θ(t2/2)(1/2) dt\int_0^\theta(t^2/2)(1/2)\,dt∫0θ​(t2/2)(1/2)dt, and the probability of being on is 1−θ/21-\theta/21−θ/2. So

EL(θ)=θ312+λ0(1−θ2),dELdθ=θ24−λ02.\mathbb E\mathcal L(\theta) =\frac{\theta^3}{12}+\lambda_0\left(1-\frac\theta2\right), \qquad \frac{d\mathbb E\mathcal L}{d\theta} =\frac{\theta^2}{4}-\frac{\lambda_0}{2}.EL(θ)=12θ3​+λ0​(1−2θ​),dθdEL​=4θ2​−2λ0​​.

The average loss has a clear minimum at θ=2λ0\theta=\sqrt{2\lambda_0}θ=2λ0​​, again 0.5 in Figure 2. Move the threshold a little and a small mass of nearby samples flips state; that probability mass provides the expected gradient.

The JumpReLU paper builds a straight-through gradient from a kernel density estimate near the threshold. The threshold pseudo-derivative contains

∂~ JumpReLU⁡θ(u)∂θ=−θεK ⁣(u−θε).\frac{\widetilde\partial\,\operatorname{JumpReLU}_\theta(u)} {\partial\theta} =-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon} K\!\left(\frac{u-\theta}{\varepsilon}\right).∂θ∂JumpReLUθ​(u)​=−εθ​K(εu−θ​).

The bandwidth ε\varepsilonε decides how wide a neighborhood counts. Larger means more samples but also cases further from the threshold; smaller is more local but can be noisy with too few samples. The scalar example here only explains why this kind of gradient estimate is needed; the formula plot isn’t a JumpReLU training result. The experiments below use TopK.

The experiment: 27 trainings with a known answer

The signal here isn’t language model activations. The generating dictionary D∗∈R8×12D_*\in\mathbb R^{8\times12}D∗​∈R8×12 has fixed random unit columns, and each of 12 non-negative features appears independently with probability 0.08, with magnitude U[0,1]U[0,1]U[0,1] when it does. Training sees only h=D∗fh=D_*fh=D∗​f; the generating dictionary and coefficients are kept as the answer key and never enter the optimizer.

Two reasons for this setup. First, “recovering a direction” has an exact meaning. Second, an input averages only 12×0.08=0.9612\times0.08=0.9612×0.08=0.96 features, so we can say concretely what happens when the sparsity budget is loosened. Real models don’t allow this full comparison, so it’s worth seeing how the metrics relate here first.

Training uses the public TopK class above, with dictionary width m=8,12,24m=8,12,24m=8,12,24 and activity cap k=1,2,4k=1,2,4k=1,2,4, three random seeds per combination, 27 runs in all. Each run is 2400 steps, with one input stream per seed; decoder columns are renormalized each step, and the encoder uses the decoder’s transpose only at initialization. No group got extra training because its results looked bad.

The test set has 8192 samples with an actual mean of 0.944458 generating features. Reconstruction error is normalized by the error of always predicting the test mean:

Erec=∑n∥hn−h^n∥22∑n∥hn−hˉ∥22.E_{\mathrm{rec}}= \frac{\sum_n\|h_n-\hat h_n\|_2^2} {\sum_n\|h_n-\bar h\|_2^2}.Erec​=∑n​∥hn​−hˉ∥22​∑n​∥hn​−h^n​∥22​​.

Direction recovery is a separate calculation. Match learned directions one-to-one against the 12 generating directions to maximize total cosine, then count the fraction of generating directions with cosine at least 0.9. Because coefficients are non-negative, opposite directions don’t count as the same, so cosines aren’t taken in absolute value. 0.9 is a geometric threshold, not a pass mark for semantic interpretation, and a match doesn’t verify coefficient recovery.

The opening result is the last two rows. At m=24,k=2m=24,k=2m=24,k=2, all three seeds recover all 12 directions. At k=4k=4k=4, reconstruction error falls from about 0.049 to 0.016, while direction recovery lands between 16.67% and 58.33%. Choosing by reconstruction error alone would pick exactly the latter.

From the objective’s point of view this isn’t surprising. The optimizer is asked to rebuild hhh accurately, not to use D∗D_*D∗​. With more directions available per sample, it can let several imperfectly aligned directions jointly represent one generating feature and use spare coefficients to patch the bias. The representation gets more flexible and reconstruction looks better, while “which latent corresponds to which generating factor” gets murkier. The test inputs average fewer than one generating feature, yet the k=4k=4k=4 SAE almost always uses all four nonzeros: the two representations no longer have the same granularity.

That isn’t a verdict against k=4k=4k=4 in general. If the data often contained four or five simultaneous features, a small kkk would force information loss. With closer generating directions, some noise or a different training budget, the best configuration could flip. What this experiment supports is one sentence: on a known generating process, lower reconstruction error did not guarantee better direction recovery.

Wider dictionaries bring one more effect. In the three m=24,k=2m=24,k=2m=24,k=2 runs, 8.33%, 16.67% and 20.83% of latents never activated anywhere in the test set. They do nothing on this finite test set, but that definition can’t be compared directly with papers’ dead-latent counts over long training sequences. With more options, recovery rate and redundancy both change; reconstruction, sparsity and utilization need to be read together.

From reconstruction to behavior

A real language model has no D∗D_*D∗​ to compare against. What you can do first is replace the original activation hhh with the reconstruction h^\hat hh^ and see whether the downstream computation still holds up.

Activation MSE can’t answer that. Suppose downstream only reads 100h1100h_1100h1​: an error of 0.01 in the first dimension changes the output by 1, while the same error in the second dimension changes nothing. A language model also has many nonlinear layers and final token probabilities, so you can’t treat reconstruction error in every direction as equally important. That’s why SAE papers measure downstream loss after substitution rather than reporting a reconstruction curve alone.

The substitution can go through an activation hook. In the pinned version of TransformerLens’s hook_points.py, HookPoint itself is just:

def forward(self, x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
    return x

User functions attach as forward hooks and return a replacement tensor. Without a hook, computation passes through unchanged; with one, the same site can cache hhh or feed h^\hat hh^ downstream. The implementation is MIT licensed.

Where you inject is part of the interpretation, too. An MLP nonlinearity’s output, an attention output and the residual stream are different computational objects even with the same dimension. A direction learned at one site can’t be moved to another just because the shapes match.

Only after whole-layer reconstruction holds up does it make sense to trace a single direction. Write

h=Dz+b+ϵ,h=Dz+b+\epsilon,h=Dz+b+ϵ,

with ϵ\epsilonϵ the SAE’s reconstruction residual. Ablating only latent jjj should give

h′=h−djzj=b+∑i≠jdizi+ϵ.h'=h-d_jz_j =b+\sum_{i\ne j}d_i z_i+\epsilon.h′=h−dj​zj​=b+i=j∑​di​zi​+ϵ.

The original residual stays. Substituting b+∑i≠jdizib+\sum_{i\ne j}d_i z_ib+∑i=j​di​zi​ instead removes the target direction and the whole residual at once. Downstream changes either way, but the two experiments support different interpretations, and writing the residual into the formula makes that impossible to overlook.

Another step is easy to skip. Seeing that a few top-activating samples all discuss the same topic, you name the direction. Naming uses up those samples’ information, so validation has to move to inputs that weren’t used for naming, actively looking for activating and non-activating cases that don’t fit the explanation. Then try strength changes, ablation or moderate intervention and see whether downstream moves as predicted. Interventions that are too large push the model off its usual states, so even if something changes, that doesn’t prove natural computation follows the same path.

Towards Monosemanticity connects sample-based explanations with intervention and discusses feature splitting as dictionaries widen. The TopK paper evaluates reconstruction, probes and downstream ablation separately. These evaluations can’t substitute for each other, for exactly the reason the opening experiment exposed: the same activation can be assembled in more than one way.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • It shows, on data with a known generating dictionary, that the SAE configuration with the lowest reconstruction error (m=24,k=4m=24,k=4m=24,k=4) recovered far fewer generating directions than a sparser one (m=24,k=2m=24,k=2m=24,k=2).
  • It shows, with closed-form scalar examples, how L1 shrinks active coefficients while L0 doesn’t, and why a hard threshold still has a well-defined expected gradient.
  • It doesn’t load a language model, name any semantic features or run real-model ablations.
  • The constructed data is noise-free, with about 37% all-zero test inputs, far from real activation distributions; the training is a fixed-budget recovery experiment, not any paper’s full recipe.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. Superposition lets a model store more features than it has dimensions, at the cost of making the decomposition ambiguous, and sparsity alone doesn’t make it unique. An SAE learns a sparse decomposition that reconstructs well; L1, TopK and JumpReLU differ in how they charge for sparsity. On data where we know the answer, the best-reconstructing decomposition wasn’t the true one. In a real model, closing that gap takes held-out validation and interventions in the original computation.

The results so far are enough to rule out one overly strong claim: an SAE reconstructing well doesn’t mean it found the model’s real internal concepts. That conclusion would need evidence of how the direction behaves on unseen inputs and what it does in the original computation.

Reconstruction tells you the dictionary can rebuild the activation; only intervention tells you the model uses it.

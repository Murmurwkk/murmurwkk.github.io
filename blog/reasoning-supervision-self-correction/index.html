From CoT to SCoRe: What Each Method Actually Supervises

Two solutions reach x = 8, one by luck. Following them through CoT, sampling, STaR, process and outcome rewards, and SCoRe shows what each method rewards and what it can't see.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 7 个章节
#CoT#process supervision#self-correction#reinforcement learning#evaluation#paper deep dive

Here’s a one-line equation: 2(x−3)=102(x-3)=102(x−3)=10. Two students solve it.

Student A writes: “Divide by 2 to get x−3=5x-3=5x−3=5, so x=8x=8x=8.” Student B writes: “Expand to get 2x=142x=142x=14, so x=8x=8x=8.” Both final answers are correct. B’s two middle steps are both wrong, and they happen to cancel out on the way back to 8.

A grader that only says “correct” can’t tell you which step it checked. Keep these two solutions in mind; we’ll run them through every system in this post.

Once a model has produced a solution, there’s more than one thing you can do with it, and the techniques all called “making models reason better” differ mainly in which one they do:

  • Sample more solutions and pick the most reliable. This doesn’t touch the weights.
  • Select with an evaluator, which only changes which solution gets submitted this time.
  • Train on the successes, which changes what gets generated next time.
  • Revise: have the model reread what it just wrote and try again, conditioned on its own text.

Figure 1: A map of this post. The top half is one round of inference; training updates and second attempts happen in different places. A second attempt can read existing text but doesn’t change parameters by itself, and the feedback may be just a generic “check your work” instruction with no correct answer in it.

Something that helps at one step isn’t guaranteed to help at another. We’ll go through them in the order the field did: CoT (prompting), self-consistency and process reward models (sampling and selection), STaR and outcome-reward RL (training), and SCoRe (revision). At each stage we’ll ask what it actually changes and what it leaves behind.

Three questions about one solution

Before any method, it helps to separate three different judgments about a solution:

  1. Does the answer satisfy the problem?
  2. Does the written derivation support the answer?
  3. Did the model actually compute by the reasoning in the text?

The first two can be checked. The third can’t be checked from the text alone. Student A and Student B agree on question 1 and differ on question 2. Long solutions with trial and backtracking need one more distinction: a failed route that’s explicitly abandoned isn’t the same failure as an error left standing in the final proof.

Stage 1: CoT changes the input, not the weights

An autoregressive model reads everything before token ttt when generating it, including what it just wrote. So intermediate text becomes input to later computation. The original CoT paper gave several “question, reasoning, answer” examples and had the model imitate them on new questions, without fine-tuning any weights (CoT paper).

Form alone isn’t enough. The paper tried outputting only equations, padding the output with meaningless dots, and moving the explanation after the final answer; on complex arithmetic none of these reliably reproduced normal CoT’s effect. What the intermediate text says, and where it goes, both matter. (One footnote: the dot-padding experiment didn’t strictly match inference FLOPs, context access and length distribution, so it can’t be used to rule out every computational mechanism.)

The same paper also hand-inspected LaMDA 137B’s GSM8K answers: of 50 samples with correct final answers, 2 had flawed reasoning. That’s a small sample, but enough to show that checking only answers lets invalid reasoning through. It isn’t an estimate that “4% of all responses have reasoning errors,” and it certainly doesn’t show the rest faithfully reflect internal computation.

Let’s write the visible generation down. With question xxx, intermediate text zzz and answer aaa, the model produces

(z,a)∼πθ(⋅∣x,p),(z,a)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,p),(z,a)∼πθ​(⋅∣x,p),

where ppp is the prompt and examples. One sample is one draw. If the first draw misses the correct trajectory, either the model almost never gets it, or the correct trajectory has some probability and this draw just missed. Those two situations call for different remedies.

Stage 2: sampling covers, selection chooses

Coverage

For a fixed question with single-sample success probability ppp, the probability of at least one success in kkk samples is

pass@⁡k=1−(1−p)k.\operatorname{pass@}k=1-(1-p)^k.pass@k=1−(1−p)k.

That measures whether the candidate set covers a correct answer. You can compute it with the reference answer after the fact. A deployed system can’t: it still has to pick one of its candidates to submit.

Take a simplified case with only two kinds of answer, right or wrong. For odd kkk, majority vote picks the right one with probability

Pvote=∑j=(k+1)/2k(kj)pj(1−p)k−j.P_{\mathrm{vote}} =\sum_{j=(k+1)/2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}p^j(1-p)^{k-j}.Pvote​=j=(k+1)/2∑k​(jk​)pj(1−p)k−j.

At p=0.4p=0.4p=0.4, more candidates make it easier for at least one to be right, and make majority vote more reliably wrong. “The correct answer appeared” and “the correct answer was chosen” are different events.

Figure 2: Computed from the binomial distribution with p=0.4p=0.4p=0.4 and 0.60.60.6; the fully correlated line copies the same answer every time. No language model was called. On open-ended math, wrong answers scatter across many values, so a correct answer under 50% can still win the plurality; real self-consistency also depends on answer normalization, tie handling and correlation between samples.

When nnn samples have been generated and ccc are correct, the coverage of kkk drawn uniformly from them is

pass@⁡k^=1−(n−ck)(nk),k≤n.\widehat{\operatorname{pass@}k} =1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom nk},\qquad k\le n.pass@k​=1−(kn​)(kn−c​)​,k≤n.

Using that to estimate a fixed generation policy also requires a sampling assumption. Changing the prompt, the temperature, or letting round two read round one doesn’t produce interchangeable independent samples.

Selection

An evaluator handles the step after the candidates. When exactly one candidate may be submitted, final success decomposes as

P(submit a correct answer)=P(a correct answer is among the candidates)P(choose correctly∣one is present).P(\text{submit a correct answer}) =P(\text{a correct answer is among the candidates}) P(\text{choose correctly}\mid\text{one is present}).P(submit a correct answer)=P(a correct answer is among the candidates)P(choose correctly∣one is present).

The second factor is conditional, so the two needn’t be independent. More sampling may raise the first factor while bringing in more wrong candidates that can fool the evaluator, so final success doesn’t necessarily rise with it.

Let’s Verify Step by Step compares exactly this stage. With the generator fixed, it selects with an outcome reward model (ORM), a process reward model (PRM) or majority vote. At best-of-1860 in Figure 3: PRM 78.2%, ORM 72.4%, majority vote 69.6%. That’s 1860 samples per question and then one choice, so it’s neither first-answer accuracy nor oracle coverage.

The data matters too. That experiment held out 500 problems from the original MATH test set for evaluation and folded the remaining 4500 into the original 7500 training problems; the base model and generator also had extra math training. Under those conditions, process evaluation genuinely improves selection. The numbers don’t transfer to the full original 5000-problem test set, and they don’t say the generator’s single-shot ability improved.

Turning step scores into one candidate score

A PRM’s labeling unit is the step, with positive, negative and neutral labels. That can point to where an error starts, instead of judging “all correct until the last calculation” as worthless. The paper’s experiments used filtered data of about 800k step labels, 75k solutions and 12k problems; the public unfiltered release is bigger, and the two shouldn’t be mixed up.

Step labels aren’t a selection rule yet. With per-step “not an error” scores qtq_tqt​ (positive and neutral merged), the paper multiplies:

s(z)=∏t=1Tqt,log⁡s(z)=∑t=1Tlog⁡qt.s(z)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_t,\qquad \log s(z)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q_t.s(z)=t=1∏T​qt​,logs(z)=t=1∑T​logqt​.

At 0.98 per step, five steps give about 0.904 and twenty steps about 0.668. Length leaks into the ranking through the aggregation rule. Only if the scores carry suitable conditional-probability and calibration meaning, with compatible step segmentation, can the product be read as the probability the whole solution is correct; ordinary classifier outputs don’t get that interpretation for free.

Taking the minimum step score instead emphasizes the weakest point, and hides the difference between one risky step and several moderate risks adding up. In the paper’s Appendix F, merging positive and neutral gives 78.2% with the product and 77.6% with the minimum; treating neutral as negative gives 77.4% and 77.8%. So a PRM isn’t “add some step labels and you’re done”: label semantics, step segmentation and aggregation jointly decide what gets picked.

Training data shapes the comparison too. The paper deliberately sent annotators samples that the evaluator liked but whose final answers were wrong, specifically to train recognition of convincing errors. The large-scale PRM and ORM differ in data construction and quantity, with separate small-scale controlled comparisons, so explaining the gain means reading each experiment under its own conditions.

Stage 3: training on your own successes (STaR)

If the model is sometimes right, instead of sampling widely every time, you can use the successful trajectories to update it. STaR turns that into iterated data generation plus supervised fine-tuning (paper).

Training data has only the question xxx and reference answer yyy, no human reasoning. The model generates reasoning and an answer, and only correct ones are kept. Failed questions can go through rationalization: put the correct answer in the prompt, have the model try to produce reasoning that reaches it, filter, and use the result as a training target with the answer hint removed for fine-tuning.

That step tells the model where the finish line is. Once it knows the destination, valid backward derivations appear, and so do explanations cobbled together to reach the given answer. Checking only the final answer still can’t tell Student A from Student B.

Outcome rewards show both why this kind of filtering works and why it’s not enough. The idealized objective is

J(θ)=E(z,a)∼πθ(⋅∣x)[1{a=y}],J(\theta)= \mathbb E_{(z,a)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)} [\mathbf1\{a=y\}],J(θ)=E(z,a)∼πθ​(⋅∣x)​[1{a=y}],

with policy gradient

∇θJ=E[1{a=y}∇θlog⁡πθ(z,a∣x)].\nabla_\theta J =\mathbb E[ \mathbf1\{a=y\}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(z,a\mid x)].∇θ​J=E[1{a=y}∇θ​logπθ​(z,a∣x)].

Correct trajectories get a signal to raise their own probability, but the reward doesn’t say which step deserved it. Valid derivations, filler and invalid steps that happened to land on the right answer all get reinforced together. Student B gets the same reward as Student A.

Real STaR also involves greedy generation, filtering, answer hints and multi-step SFT, re-fine-tuning from the initial model each round. It isn’t an unbiased on-policy estimate of that gradient. The formula explains why successes provide a direction; which way you actually go depends on how data is generated and filtered.

With VVV meaning the reasoning is valid and AAA meaning the final answer is correct, the share of valid reasoning after filtering is

P(V∣A)=P(A∣V)P(V)P(A∣V)P(V)+P(A∣¬V)P(¬V).P(V\mid A)= \frac{P(A\mid V)P(V)} {P(A\mid V)P(V)+P(A\mid\neg V)P(\neg V)}.P(V∣A)=P(A∣V)P(V)+P(A∣¬V)P(¬V)P(A∣V)P(V)​.

As long as invalid reasoning can also be right, filtered data isn’t clean process supervision. That’s especially easy to overlook when the answer space is small, there are option cues, or the grader only checks one number. Conversely, valuable reasoning that fails at the last step gets thrown out entirely, so data drifts toward problems the original model already found easy.

The paper’s GPT-J 6B results show exactly this “starting success rate” effect:

CommonsenseQA is five-way multiple choice with a 20% random baseline; GSM8K starts much lower, so far fewer trajectories survive filtering. The table shows best results near iteration saturation, and the methods’ training subsets and iteration budgets aren’t fully aligned, so it’s not an equal-budget comparison.

STaR’s contribution is that a few successful trajectories can improve the generation distribution. The problem it leaves is just as direct: how do problems where the model almost never succeeds get into training? Answer hints help, at the cost of handing “was the reasoning valid?” back to verification.

Stage 4: outcome-reward RL, sharpening vs. coverage

Moving from filtered SFT to outcome-reward training doesn’t make the coverage problem go away. DeepSeek-R1 shows what large-scale verifiable-reward training can do. In the paper’s first version, R1-Zero and full R1 have different supervision sources: R1-Zero skips the initial SFT but still relies on pretraining plus answer and format rewards, while full R1 adds cold start, RL, rejection sampling and SFT (R1 v1).

If first-answer accuracy rises after training, there are two possible reasons. Problems the model occasionally solved became reliable, or problems it almost never solved became solvable. Both are useful, and you need different metrics to see them.

A constructed example shows they can move in opposite directions. Split problems into two equal groups. Before training, single-sample success is (0.9,0.01)(0.9,0.01)(0.9,0.01); after, (0.99,0)(0.99,0)(0.99,0). Mean first-answer accuracy rises from 45.5% to 49.5%. But with 256 independent samples allowed, coverage goes from about 96.2% before to only about 50% after. Training raised success on the easy group and erased the rare successes on the other. (These are given probabilities, not a trained model, but they’re enough to show two metrics moving in opposite directions.)

Figure 3: Left, coverage before and after the constructed “training.” Right, the probability that a sampled group contains both successes and failures.

Yue et al. show a separation in a real configuration. Qwen2.5-7B trained on 2000 Omni-MATH-Rule problems, with 821 for in-domain test and MATH500 for out-of-domain test. In Table 4, in-domain pass@1 goes from 10.2% to 28.3% after 450 GRPO steps, while pass@256 drops from 69.1% to 63.9%. On the other hand, MATH500 pass@256 at 150 steps is 97.2%, above the base model’s 96.2%. The effect varies with task and training stage, so it doesn’t follow that RL always shrinks coverage (paper v4).

“Wrong all 256 times” also doesn’t mean a correct solution doesn’t exist in the distribution. For a fixed problem with independent samples and zero successes, the one-sided 95% upper bound is

pupper=1−0.051/256≈1.163%.p_{\mathrm{upper}}=1-0.05^{1/256}\approx1.163\%.pupper​=1−0.051/256≈1.163%.

If the true success rate is just 0.1%, the chance of 256 straight failures is still about 77.4%. A finite budget measures observable coverage; it can’t be promoted to a strict capability boundary, and screening many problems adds multiple-comparison and selection bias on top.

Grading rules rewrite coverage too. Wen et al. require both a correct answer and reasoning that passes a check, comparing CoT-Pass@K for Qwen2.5-32B and DAPO-Qwen-32B, with DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B as the reasoning evaluator and any/all/majority over repeated judgments (paper v2). That adds an observation dimension beyond answers, and also brings in evaluator error. It isn’t formal verification, and it doesn’t measure whether internal computation matches the text.

When every sample in a group is wrong

Common group-relative rewards sample GGG solutions per problem and build, from binary rewards,

Ai=ri−rˉσr+ε.A_i=\frac{r_i-\bar r}{\sigma_r+\varepsilon}.Ai​=σr​+εri​−rˉ​.

If the whole group is wrong, or all right, the numerator is zero. For this advantage term there’s nothing to compare within the group. (Other problems, KL or auxiliary losses can still produce gradients, so it’s not that the whole training step does nothing.)

Under independent sampling with single-sample success ppp, the probability a group contains both successes and failures is

Pmixed=1−(1−p)G−pG.P_{\mathrm{mixed}}=1-(1-p)^G-p^G.Pmixed​=1−(1−p)G−pG.

That’s the right panel of Figure 3. Very hard problems are usually all wrong, very easy ones usually all right, and only the middle band reliably provides outcome contrast. And that’s only whether a signal exists, not whether the reward gets attributed to valid reasoning.

Cai et al.’s curriculum RL builds on this with a loop of probing, teacher-SFT bridging and curriculum GRPO. It estimates current difficulty, then uses a few teacher trajectories to push the model into a region where it produces successes (preprint v1). Here the teacher genuinely provides method, so the gain can’t all be credited to curriculum order. Separating contributions would require comparing teacher SFT, then uniform RL, then curriculum RL, at the same problems and teacher-token budget, with the cost of difficulty probing included.

This is the same starting point as STaR’s answer hints. Filtering or relative rewards alone can hardly squeeze a success signal out of trajectories that always fail. External information changes that, but it has to be counted as part of the training process.

Stage 5: making “check again” a training task (SCoRe)

All the candidates so far were generated independently. Revision is different: round two sees round one, and the new trajectory is conditioned on the old text. Sometimes it finds the mistake, sometimes it gets anchored to the original answer, and sometimes it breaks an answer that was right.

The arithmetic of revision

Let first-round accuracy be aaa, the fraction of original errors fixed be uuu, and the fraction of original correct answers broken be vvv. Second-round accuracy is

a2=a(1−v)+(1−a)u,a_2=a(1-v)+(1-a)u,a2​=a(1−v)+(1−a)u,

with net change

a2−a=(1−a)u−av.a_2-a=(1-a)u-av.a2​−a=(1−a)u−av.

Report only “how many errors got fixed” and the second term disappears. The stronger round one is, the fewer errors there are to fix and the more correct answers there are to break.

Figure 4: The four transitions: right→right, right→wrong, wrong→right, wrong→wrong.

Construct 100 paired records: 49 right→right, 21 right→wrong, 18 wrong→right, 12 wrong→wrong. The repair rate is 18/30=60%18/30=60\%18/30=60%, which sounds great, and final accuracy drops from 70% to 67%.

Figure 5: The constructed 100-record example.

An oracle that knows the reference and keeps the correct one of the two rounds would get 88 right; just submitting round two gets 67. The 21-question gap is exactly the broken first answers. Capturing that gain requires the system to learn when to keep and when to change; after-the-fact selection can’t be credited to the generator.

Why “teach it to fix wrong answers” isn’t enough

It seems you could just collect an old model’s wrong first answers, pair them with correct revisions, and train. But at test time the first answer comes from the current trained model, with a different error distribution. Offline training conditions on

y1∼πold(⋅∣x),y2∼πθ(⋅∣x,y1,pcheck),y_1\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid x), \qquad y_2\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y_1,p_{\mathrm{check}}),y1​∼πold​(⋅∣x),y2​∼πθ​(⋅∣x,y1​,pcheck​),

while at test time both rounds come from the current policy. Fixing fixed first answers well doesn’t guarantee fixing the current model’s own first answers well. SCoRe’s Figure 5 compares exactly these two conditions.

Another shortcut is to copy the first answer as closely as possible. That never breaks anything, and almost never fixes anything either. Some Pair-SFT configurations with right-to-right trajectories in the paper show zero flips in both directions and identical two-round accuracy. Raising round two’s accuracy alone doesn’t necessarily reward useful revision.

SCoRe’s first stage raises round-two reward while putting a strong KL constraint on round one, keeping it near the base model so training doesn’t prematurely eliminate the states that “need fixing.” Round two has its usual KL regularization too, so the shorthand formula shouldn’t be read as unconstrained.

The second stage optimizes both rounds jointly, with a progress reward. With binary outcome rewards r1,r2r_1,r_2r1​,r2​:

r2′=r2+α(r2−r1).r'_2=r_2+\alpha(r_2-r_1).r2′​=r2​+α(r2​−r1​).

With the experiments’ α=10\alpha=10α=10:

Wrong→right11
Right→right1

Fixing earns a big reward; breaking a correct answer gets a heavy penalty. But it matters which round this reward updates. Treating r1+r2′=(1−α)r1+(1+α)r2r_1+r'_2=(1-\alpha)r_1+(1+\alpha)r_2r1​+r2′​=(1−α)r1​+(1+α)r2​ as round one’s return would give the odd result of a negative coefficient on a correct first answer. The paper’s Appendix A.2 uses per-round immediate rewards, effectively γ=0\gamma=0γ=0, so round two’s progress term isn’t fed into round one’s policy gradient as future return.

Immediate rewards don’t make the rounds independent, though: parameters are shared, so round-two updates still change future first answers. That’s why the stage constraints and ablations matter. In Table 4, a model without multi-turn training gets 61.8% on the first answer and then drops to 59.4% after checking. Removing stage one gives 59.2%→61.4%, removing the progress reward gives 60.0%→62.6%, and the full method gives 60.0%→64.4%. A configuration with a stronger first answer didn’t automatically become a better reviser.

During training, rewards come from reference answers or program tests. At test time, round two has only its own response and a generic check instruction, with no indication of whether round one was right. “Intrinsic self-correction” describes the test-time feedback condition; it doesn’t mean training had no external supervision. The code task switches to Gemini 1.0 Pro, trained on MBPP and evaluated on HumanEval, so it can’t be combined with the math table as one model’s improvement. And extending to ten rounds doesn’t improve forever: a policy trained for two rounds hits new state distributions if it keeps revising.

Is revision worth the budget?

Learning to revise isn’t the end. Round two costs new generation and a longer context. And the alternative isn’t only “stop now”; you could generate another independent sample and use the same selector.

SCoRe §6.2 compares generating 2K2K2K in parallel against generating KKK and revising each once, both with self-consistency. In that setting, spending part of the candidate budget on revision pays off. But equal candidate counts aren’t equal tokens or FLOPs, and round two’s input cost is larger (§6.2).

To test this on a specific model, keep first answers on a batch of problems unused in training and run three branches: submit directly; resample at the same total token budget; revise while reading the first answer. Fix the selector and grader in advance, and log input and output tokens separately. If revision beats the first answer but loses to same-budget resampling, the extra compute is genuinely useful, but the self-correction structure hasn’t shown an advantage yet.

The unit of comparison should be the problem. Generating many times for one problem doesn’t turn it into many independent problems; keeping the pairing and aggregating by problem shows which problems a strategy actually changed. Disagreement cases are the most useful: a right first answer broken by revision, a right answer with wrong reasoning, a high evaluator score on a wrong choice. They point directly at whether to fix generation, selection or revision next.

Conclusion

From CoT to SCoRe, each method brought a different position into training. CoT made intermediate text part of generation. Sampling and selection separated coverage from choice, and PRMs judged candidates at the step level. STaR and outcome-reward RL fed successes back into the weights, sharpening what the model could already do and sometimes trading away rare successes. SCoRe trained the model to face its own first answer. No single stage’s accuracy explains the others.

So run Student B’s solution, the one with two wrong steps and a right answer, through each system. CoT lets it be written. Self-consistency may vote for it. An ORM rewards it, while a PRM may catch it at step one. STaR trains on it. RL reinforces it alongside Student A. And SCoRe asks whether, on a second read, the model would notice. Each answer is different.

A correct final answer is one bit of evidence; every method in this post is a different way of deciding what that bit should be allowed to teach.

Environment and measurement method

No language model was trained or sampled locally; every model performance number is the original paper’s result at the linked version, checked against its appendices. Locally, only numerical scripts were run: NumPy generates constructed data for paired transitions, binomial sampling, step products and group rewards from the formulas, and recomputes the net gains in SCoRe Tables 2 and 4.

python bench_mur/systems/reasoning_metric_evidence.py

Materials are in rep_mur/blog-rewrite/systems/reasoning/, including inputs, CSVs, transcribed paper tables and versions.

Reproduction advice and source limits

If you run your own model experiments, save problem IDs and splits, model and prompt, temperature and length limits, full text of every round, visible feedback, input and output tokens, the grader and the final choice. Paired tables must keep both rounds, not just summary accuracy, or you can’t recover where fixes and breaks came from.

The local PDFs of the original CoT and STaR papers don’t include all external appendices, so the available material isn’t enough to rebuild full training recipes. The PRM and SCoRe methods, tables and appendices used here were read and checked at the versions linked in the text.

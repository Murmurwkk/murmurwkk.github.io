Right Floor, Wrong Height: A Barometer in Indoor Navigation

All 848 reference points had the right floor, while one route's height spanned 20 m without changing floors. How pressure became continuous height inside the shared filter, and what each fix cost.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 19 个章节
#barometer#floor estimation#inertial navigation#factor graphs#error attribution

The result that first convinced us the floor module was in good shape was a very pretty one: across all 72 logs, every one of 848 reference points matched its floor. When we went back and checked later, the same historical outputs contained a route labeled F3 the whole time whose height spanned 6.541 m, and another route that never changed floors whose height spanned 20.207 m.

Those two results don’t contradict each other. The first checks which floor the person is on; the second checks how the trajectory’s vertical coordinate behaves. At the time, we’d only really solved the first.

What came next wasn’t simply “plug the barometer into the graph and we’re done.” First, horizontal correction tilted the route into a slope. Then, with pressure constraints added, real stair climbs still got flattened. After we fixed the statistical meaning of historical states, multi-meter sawtooth artifacts disappeared, while some horizontal reference points got worse. Only once the pressure reference, time-correlated noise, inertial state and history revisions were all put into one computation could we start telling which problems came from the sensor, which from the motion model, and which were just software mixing up two different meanings of “history.”

This post follows those problems in the order they appeared, got pulled apart by evidence, and were changed. The order reflects the development process; it doesn’t mean each later version beats the earlier one on every metric. The text doesn’t keep a dated diary; times in the figures are relative seconds within each log, kept so different results can be compared over the same stretch of motion.

Here’s the route through it:

  • Part 1: the original floor-change design, the pressure reference, transition events, and why the old acceptance tests missed height drift.
  • Part 2: pressure enters the continuous trajectory for the first time: finite rotations, shared-reference correlation and sampling coverage.
  • Part 3: why real stairs got flattened: Motion’s vertical response, the semantics of historical states, and what the fix cost.
  • Part 4: building a shared pressure posterior: feedback into the future, bounded noise memory, and re-running the real network.
  • Part 5: the full history exposes new problems, and the fix meets the runtime: same-floor dips, horizontal jumps, releasing old nodes, an old gate blocking the right posterior, lost startup references, the full control run and its performance limits.
  • Part 6: how the pieces fit, the reusable lessons, what’s still unsolved, and the evidence index.

On narrow screens, wide tables and flowcharts scroll horizontally within their own area. Data figures keep their original resolution and can be zoomed; key numbers are also listed in the tables.

Before we start: what was actually measured

Before getting to algorithms, we need to separate five kinds of data that often get mixed up.

NameWhat it actually isWhat it can answerWhat it can’t answer directly
Raw PRESPressure samples recorded by the phoneWhether and when pressure changedWhich floor the person is on, or the actual altitude
Pressure relative heightHeight change converted relative to some pressure referenceUnder the model’s assumptions, what rise or fall pressure supportsIndependent, unbiased continuous height ground truth
FloorThe floor number committed by a state machine or discrete graphWhich floor is currently confirmedWhether the continuous U coordinate within that floor is right

EN here means east and north; U means up. The logs are trials from the IPIN dataset, and we name them by what happens in them: the stairs walk (T25, session01/SA52) changes floor six times; the flat-floor walk (T31, session02/SA52) and a second flat-floor walk (T32) stay on one floor; and the control walk (T21) is the run used for the runtime checks in Part 5. Some figures still label them by those short IDs. The four flat-floor snapshots later on are four historical solve segments of the same log; they can’t be treated as four independent validation logs, and they shouldn’t be confused with the A/B/C segments used for candidate construction and confirmation in the long chain.

A few metrics need agreeing on up front, too:

  • Height span is the maximum minus the minimum within one evaluation range; height net change is the last point minus the first. They’re not the same number.
  • Pressure residual RMS describes how compatible the algorithm is with pressure observations; it isn’t an independent height error.
  • Horizontal errors are only directly comparable at the same physical times on the same set of reference points.
  • Matrix differences between algorithms near machine precision prove the implementations agree under the same approximation, not that the trajectory is close to how a person actually walked.

We don’t have dense independent height ground truth. For same-floor walking, “body sway plus raising and lowering the phone shouldn’t produce meters, let alone tens of meters, of rise and fall” is an important physical check. The roughly 1.5 m empirical range we once proposed could only ever be used to spot problems. It was never a condition for clipping output, rejecting trajectories, picking the best solution or back-solving noise weights, and it certainly doesn’t apply to stair segments.

Part 1: From pressure to floors

The first task: turning pressure changes into floor candidates

Pressure gives relative change, and the reference isn’t a throwaway constant

Under a model where local temperature is roughly fixed, pressure converts to relative height as

hi=−slog⁡PiP0,s=RairTg.h_i=-s\log\frac{P_i}{P_0},\qquad s=\frac{R_{\mathrm{air}}T}{g}.hi​=−slogP0​Pi​​,s=gRair​T​.

PiP_iPi​ is the current pressure, P0P_0P0​ the reference pressure, TTT the assumed temperature and sss a conversion scale in meters. When pressure drops, converted height rises. Android’s altitude API likewise requires a reference pressure, and its docs distinguish absolute altitude from the relative height difference between two readings (Android altitude API).

The development model we kept using takes T=293.15 KT=293.15\,\mathrm{K}T=293.15K, giving s≈8581.016 ms\approx8581.016\,\mathrm{m}s≈8581.016m. Near 1000 hPa, a small 1 hPa change corresponds to about 8.58 m. That’s only the approximate sensitivity at that temperature and local pressure, not a fixed floor-height conversion table for every building.

This also clears up two common misunderstandings. First, Pa and hPa cancel in the ratio if numerator and denominator use the same unit, so changing units alone won’t fix a height that goes the wrong way. Second, taking a ratio removes a common multiplicative scale, but it doesn’t automatically remove additive bias, time-varying weather pressure, indoor–outdoor pressure differences or slow sensor drift. A more realistic expression is

zh(t)=h(t)+b(t)+ϵ(t).z_h(t)=h(t)+b(t)+\epsilon(t).zh​(t)=h(t)+b(t)+ϵ(t).

Here b(t)b(t)b(t) holds the not-yet-explained baseline change. If the person stands still and b(t)b(t)b(t) changes, pressure height changes anyway. If the person is climbing while ambient pressure moves the other way, the real rise can be partly cancelled. So a pressure trend is evidence, not a floor label with the answer already attached.

From picking a floor every frame to tracking a whole transition

The early repository had several attempts side by side: barometric height, moving averages, gradients, WiFi floor voting. A mixed graph later alternated between solving discrete floors and continuous height, but in real runs it bounced between F3, F4 and F5 and had trouble converging. More iterations or looser numerical tolerances let some runs get further, without answering the actual question: why should this pressure change count as one floor change?

The clearer direction that followed organizes a floor change as a process with a start, a direction and an end. On a flat floor, maintain a reference. When a trend appears, open a candidate. The candidate accumulates pressure and activity evidence over time and proposes a transition between legal adjacent floors. Once confirmed, the completed event is locked, so fluctuations on a landing can’t keep generating the same transition again.

This design doesn’t require the person to stop completely to change floors. What it separates is “there’s enough evidence to change the floor number” from “the body has reached the stair exit.” The first can hold mid-climb; the second also needs a landing, the entry–exit relationship and map connectivity.

Over time this division of responsibilities took shape. The diagram below summarizes how the pieces relate; the box names aren’t a complete enumeration from the source.

flowchart TD

Activity recognition offers a motion explanation here, but it doesn’t own the final answer. Missing STEP events don’t prove someone is standing still, and elevators don’t need step events at all. Some WALKING labels in Floor just mean “not recognized as vertical activity” and can’t be treated as strict walking confirmation. And the same IMU being read by several modules doesn’t turn it into several independent pieces of evidence.

What this stage achieved was making floor changes correspond to a traceable physical process. It hadn’t yet constrained continuous height.

Why “every floor correct” didn’t expose the height problem

When re-checking history, you can’t just read “passed” in a report title; you have to look at the denominator and what was actually evaluated.

Two older batches over all 72 logs really did each record all 848 reference points matching their floor. That includes 72 startup points, leaving 776 after removing them. It’s a valuable historical result, and finding height problems later doesn’t erase it. But reference points only occur at sparse times. That statistic neither checks continuous height frame by frame nor proves there were no wrong floor changes that switched back in between.

Before those two results, a 40-log development report already had a process health rate of 40/40, yet only 29/40 logs had every floor completely correct. Reference points were 489/516, about 94.77%, with 3 extra transitions, 34 missed transitions and 1 reversed transition recorded. It shows clearly that “the software didn’t crash” and “the floor calls were right” are different metrics. Point-level errors and event categories can’t simply be added together either.

A more direct counterexample comes from same-floor trajectories:

Figure 1: Each case keeps its own statistical range. The figure shows an evaluation gap; it doesn’t compare algorithms by the spans of different routes.

The reason at the code level is specific. The floor module at the time already used pressure to detect vertical events, but the historical run configuration we checked explicitly turned off the continuous barometer factor and height_floor coupling, while turning on floor_transition_episode. PRES showing up in the input list only proves some module read pressure, not that pressure constrained the U of every graph node.

The history also really does contain same-floor logs that gained an extra floor change and then rolled back and recovered. Later audits also found some reruns covered only part of the logs or lacked usable position output. Compress partial regressions, full datasets and structure-only passes into one sentence like “it never got anything wrong before,” and attribution is distorted from the very start.

So we separated floor acceptance from height acceptance. Floors are checked on reference points, wrong transitions and delay. Height is checked on same-floor shape, real rises and falls, and state before and after updates. Navigation quality then checks horizontal position and velocity. The three tables can explain each other, but none can replace another.

Half-floor false commits on long routes

In a later saved investigation of the official long logs, the activity path proposed transitions at about half a floor’s height, for responsiveness. A nominal floor height of 3.5 m, a progress ratio of 0.50 and a tolerance of 0.02 give a proposal scale of about 1.68 m. Some false commits happened at about 1.68–1.97 m, while early proposals during real climbs fell in a similar range.

That’s not a classification problem a stricter height threshold is guaranteed to fix. Continuing up after reaching half a floor and turning back after reaching half a floor can provide similar evidence in the first half. Commit too early with no physical recheck and no way to retract, and one mistake can leave every later correct transition carrying a one-floor offset. The interface originally meant to reject uncommitted candidates can’t simply take on the job of undoing a committed floor, either.

Waiting for a full floor’s height has its own costs, though. Historical research recorded missed detections on dense back-and-forth and short landing stops, and new events being masked while waiting for a recheck. A pressure level-shift fallback helps where activity recognition falls short, but purely replacing the activity path makes it insensitive to floors with short stops. What needs designing is the whole lifecycle (candidate, commit, recheck, retract, next event), not one threshold made responsible for both sensitivity and final correctness.

This uses the saved long-log investigation; some of the per-frame originals are no longer local, so we can’t claim it was reproduced this time. Another historical batch named full72 actually completed only 54 logs, with the other 18 failing. All 657 points with receipts are correct, but that can’t be reported as 72 logs passing. These boundaries are part of the development lesson.

Part 2: Pressure as a continuous height measurement

The first serious failure: horizontal correction tilted the 3D route

The problem really moved to the center during WiFi correction research on the flat-floor walk. Finite rotations had fixed the horizontal stretching caused by first-order large-angle approximations, and the 2D plot already looked much better, yet the 3D height still made no sense.

One snapshot had a within-segment height span of about 9.76 m. Further fitting found the whole route carried a common tilt of about 21.24∘21.24^\circ21.24∘. That rigid tilt alone predicts a height span of about 9.84 m, with a shape RMS difference of only about 0.083 m. It’s mainly one tilted ramp, not high-frequency barometric noise.

Why would 2D radio affect height? Because the navigation solve adjusts position, velocity and attitude together. Radio observes EN directly, while Motion residuals preserve relative motion. If the graph’s approximation doesn’t keep the correct world vertical fixed, tilting the whole 3D route can change its horizontal projection and thereby reduce radio residuals.

“TLIO used gravity” doesn’t close the question. Information passing from the raw front end into the graph goes through state selection, linearization, covariance projection and history compression. You have to look at what the current objective actually still contains, rather than assume from module names that gravity fully constrains every degree of freedom.

Another effect was a common upward shift of the whole segment’s height baseline. That snapshot’s first-node U moved about 15.41 m, a different mechanism from the 9.76 m within-segment rise and fall. In the boundary prior, position, velocity and attitude are correlated; when attitude changes a lot, position’s conditional mean changes too. In the original record, the common height shift matched this conditional response to about 5.4×10−125.4\times10^{-12}5.4×10−12 m.

That isn’t a “bad correlation” you can just delete. Deleting it changes the information the original prior expresses. What’s really needed is to recover enough history, check where finite rotation and the original linearized prior stay valid, and add a real vertical measurement. At large angles, a local Gaussian prior can’t uniquely determine the right nonlinear distribution far from the original linearization point.

This also corrected an intuition: the gyroscope isn’t a reference that directly keeps height from drifting. It measures angular rate; height depends on attitude, gravity removal, accelerometer bias, velocity and integration. Android’s sensor definitions explicitly separate angular rate from acceleration including gravity (Android motion sensors). Even with a smooth angular-rate curve, persistent acceleration error can integrate into a large height error. As a pure order-of-magnitude example, a constant 0.01 m/s20.01\,\mathrm{m/s^2}0.01m/s2 residual acceleration over one minute accumulates about 18 m of position error; that isn’t this project’s measurement of any phone’s bias.

Adding pressure as a continuous height measurement for the first time

Constrain relative height, don’t pin the output to a floor

We kept the raw pressure, aggregated it over causal windows, and built a relative height residual:

ri=(ui−u0)−(hi−h0).r_i=(u_i-u_0)-(h_i-h_0).ri​=(ui​−u0​)−(hi​−h0​).

It constrains the height change between two times. The unknown common altitude baseline cancels, while the starting physical U still comes from the original startup information. After solving, nothing is re-zeroed, and the whole floor’s trajectory isn’t squashed onto one fixed height.

That distinction matters. Tell the optimizer directly that “every same-floor point must lie in one plane,” and you’ll get a lovely flat line, possibly by erasing hand-held changes, ramps and steps, or even pushing a person back down mid-climb before the discrete floor has been committed. A pressure factor should express the change the sensor supports and its uncertainty, not write the desired picture into the objective.

Differences that share one reference aren’t independent

Suppose each raw height noise ϵi\epsilon_iϵi​ is independent with variance σi2\sigma_i^2σi2​. The difference noise is ϵi−ϵ0\epsilon_i-\epsilon_0ϵi​−ϵ0​. Two different differences both contain the same ϵ0\epsilon_0ϵ0​, so

Cov⁡(ϵi−ϵ0,ϵj−ϵ0)={σi2+σ02,i=j,σ02,i≠j.\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_0,\epsilon_j-\epsilon_0) =\begin{cases} \sigma_i^2+\sigma_0^2,&i=j,\\ \sigma_0^2,&i\ne j. \end{cases}Cov(ϵi​−ϵ0​,ϵj​−ϵ0​)={σi2​+σ02​,σ02​,​i=j,i=j.​

Even with independent raw samples, the difference covariance has off-diagonal terms. If raw samples are themselves time-correlated, with original covariance CCC and difference operator DDD, use DCDTDCD^\mathsf{T}DCDT.

Ignoring these terms amounts to splitting the error of one reference pressure into many independent pieces of evidence. The more observations, the more falsely certain the system becomes. The “pressure memory” that came later exists precisely to keep that shared information, not to give the algorithm some mysterious extra state.

The initial correlated noise came from the empirical autocovariance of the flat-floor walk’s early segment, fixed before solving. It isn’t an independent cross-device calibration and can’t inherit any “certified coverage” claim. But at least it states clearly where the noise came from, over what time range it was estimated, and which correlations must enter the computation.

From one pressure point every four seconds to one per second

The first full-history run with four-second pressure observations already brought all four snapshots’ full-history height spans down to about 1.37–1.58 m. Then we noticed a brief dip near 83 s, falling exactly between two pressure samples, which the raw pressure at that time didn’t support.

So the next change shortened the coverage interval of real observations while checking that raw sample blocks didn’t repeat. The matching noise autocovariance was rebuilt by the same rule: 33 early-segment samples for the four-second protocol and 129 for the one-second protocol, with zero-lag variances of about 0.041738 and 0.032577 m² respectively. This isn’t a single-variable experiment that changes only the sampling interval with every other matrix byte-identical. It didn’t tune a multiplier toward a target height, and it didn’t force the result to land “just under 1.5 m.”

The more important counterexample: a bad initialization of the same model can also produce a tiny height range while its horizontal error is about seventeen meters. Smooth height doesn’t guarantee a correct route. Multi-start solutions should be chosen by a fixed objective and convergence rule, not by picking the prettiest one by reference-point error or height range.

Great horizontal results still don’t mean the pressure problem is solved

Next, a Student-t radio likelihood brought clear correction gains on several flat-floor segments. It’s worth being clear about how that relates to the barometer: Student-t changes the tail influence of radio position residuals, while the pressure model still expresses vertical change independently. It isn’t a master switch that automatically detects and fixes pressure anomalies.

They don’t show the whole continuous run is that accurate, let alone that the same Motion–pressure combination stays reasonable when there are real stair climbs. It was the stairs walk, later on, that overturned the inference “flat floors are already good, so stairs should naturally be fine.”

Fix coordinate initialization first: map alignment mustn’t change world vertical

A later continuous-source check found a problem different from the 21.24∘21.24^\circ21.24∘ tilt above: the original map alignment itself tilted world vertical by about 2.814∘2.814^\circ2.814∘. The former was the correction solve choosing a ramp for lack of vertical information; the latter was coordinate initialization mixing in a tilt it shouldn’t have. They can’t be merged into one bug.

After restricting that alignment to a finite yaw rotation about world vertical, the no-pressure U span in the same comparison dropped from about 5.611 m to 3.256 m. It didn’t remove every height problem, but it showed some of the anomaly came from coordinate conventions. A legitimate map-heading adjustment should keep the up axis and not take the opportunity to change the gravity direction. And the same-source information from the original AHRS, IMU and TLIO can’t, on that basis, be stacked again as an independent high-precision attitude observation.

A brief dip that exposed fake input unrelated to pressure

Before changing the noise model any further, a re-check of the flat-floor walk’s input timeline found a basic problem. The app received ACCE at intervals of about 0.572 s, while neighboring real sample times were only about 0.020 s apart; GYRO had a similar gap between sample clock and arrival clock. The batch for the 82 s request was empty, yet the old preprocessing held the previous real reading and filled 100 rows of apparently covered 200 Hz input between 81.500 and 81.995 s. The 81 real IMU rows that arrived afterwards landed in a consumption window already occupied by those held values.

In that interval, propagation dropped vertical velocity by about 0.7727 m/s, and the learned update restored only about 0.00325 m/s. Tuning the barometer low-pass or pressure weight at that point wouldn’t have touched the actual cause.

The fix required building blocks from real sample support while keeping true availability times. When late input needs waiting for, wait explicitly. Held values can’t be disguised as sample coverage, and offline analysis can’t use data before it would have arrived. In a controlled source comparison, the first 27 packets and 249 updates stayed identical, and the maximum wait for real-coverage blocks afterwards was one second. That local evidence doesn’t mean every later latency problem is solved, but it makes clear why “check that the data really arrived” has to come before tuning noise.

Part 3: Real stairs

Converged, yet the person still can’t climb

The stairs walk contains real climbs and descents. We first fixed the two given exit regions, confirming that region constraints could provide horizontal correction in the shared model, and then transferred the flat-floor pressure model. The pressure scale, autocovariance and multiplier were not re-chosen for the stairs walk; it used 99 real one-second pressure windows.

The result is easy to misread as progress. Pressure residual RMS dropped from about 2.09 m to 1.04 m, horizontal error fell slightly, and the solve met the original convergence criterion. But the vertical story over the whole run was still wrong.

In both the old and new Floor audits of the same stairs walk, all six floor commits happened at exactly the same times: F2→F3 at 73.0 s, F3→F2 at 95.5 s, F2→F3 at 115.0 s, F3→F2 at 134.0 s, F2→F3 at 153.5 s, and F3→F2 at 172.5 s. This round of the continuous-height comparison covered only the earlier part of that motion. At least in this case, “a real climb got flattened” can’t be explained by the state machine suddenly producing different floor changes.

This isn’t “not enough iterations.” Convergence means the solver has found a stable compromise under the current objective. If the objective’s motion model is too confident that there’s no rise, no number of iterations will conjure up the real stairs.

Ruling out sign, unit, coordinate and timing errors

The raw pressure fell from 1024.105 to 1023.82945 mbar, so the converted height should go up. Checks confirmed the formula’s sign is right, pressure units are consistent, and the continuous reference isn’t secretly swapped at each floor. Map-heading initialization also rotates only about world vertical, with source U and transformed U differing on the order of 5×10−145\times10^{-14}5×10−14 m, so this failure can’t be blamed on coordinate alignment leaking horizontal into height.

Keeping full correlations avoids double counting, but it doesn’t make the covariance cover the network’s systematic mismatch. A mathematically correct implementation of a model can still be overconfident in a wrong motion explanation.

Separating propagation from network updates, instead of naming a culprit offhand

We read the actual state before and after each update and split the change into “propagation” and “learned update.” This is an accounting decomposition of one actual execution, not a counterfactual trajectory with the network turned off.

In 106.5–129.5 s, the roughly +0.0206 m comes from −0.0944 m of propagation and +0.1150 m of updates. There’s no evidence here for “the IMU had already correctly risen 2.5 m, and then one network update erased it all.” In 141.5–158.5 s, by contrast, propagation gives about +1.7294 m and updates about −0.8168 m, so network updates really did suppress part of the rise. Different stretches can’t be forced into the same story.

The network predicts one second of displacement every 0.05 s, with heavily overlapping output windows, so not all predictions can be summed. Fixing a choice of non-overlapping windows with full support, the 22 middle windows give a learned U total of about +0.201 m, and the 16 end windows about +0.079 m. Other phases were checked too, to avoid picking the most favorable slice. That supports “the network’s vertical response is weak,” but it isn’t enough to pin the blame uniquely on accelerometer bias, gravity projection or the training domain.

Dropping learned U exposed a more basic history-semantics bug

The sensible next comparison was to keep 3D IMU propagation, let only the EN component of the learned displacement take part in updates, and have real pressure provide the vertical observation. EN-only here is a choice about the learned measurement; it doesn’t reduce navigation to 2D, and it doesn’t force U to be constant.

That change had to be regenerated from the source. A posterior that has already fused learned U can’t become “not fused with U” by deleting one row and column of the covariance. The acceptance process needs checking too: if a 3D NIS still decides whether to accept observations, U is still indirectly influencing measurement selection. The first comparison froze the original acceptance decisions, so it isolates the effect of the update components, but it isn’t a fully redesigned 2D measurement system.

With EN-only, one-second pressure nodes fit better, yet the half-second states between two pressure nodes still showed multi-meter spikes. Near 139 s, a midpoint was off by about −2.21 m, of which about −2.39 m already existed in the source history, with finite rotation contributing only about 0.00013 m. In another adjacent half second, the saved position dropped about 10.22 m, the learned update jumped about −10.23 m, and propagation contributed only about +0.0067 m.

Connect those points directly as a physical trajectory and you’ve drawn a person dropping ten meters in half a second, with endpoint velocities that don’t support any such motion. Adding pressure weight or low-passing velocity at that point would both miss the real problem.

”What was published” and “what we now think happened” aren’t the same trajectory

The old source kept the estimates already published at each time. As later data arrived, historical means were frozen, and only part of their error correlation with the current state was maintained. That’s useful for tracking the error of old publications, but it isn’t “the joint estimate of past physical states given all current data.”

Here’s an intuitive example that doesn’t use real experiment numbers. While standing still, the old estimate thinks height is 0 m; a new observation arrives and the current estimate revises to 1 m. Connect the old 0 m to the new 1 m and the plot shows a rise. In reality it may just be a revision between two estimates, not the person moving. To draw a jointly cut-off physical history, new information has to be allowed to revise the past too.

Our mistake then wasn’t forgetting to save all the covariances. It was that the statistical object being saved and the one being consumed didn’t match. History was maintained as “frozen publication error,” but Motion edges were built as if it were “the physical trajectory under one data cutoff.” However precisely the correlations are computed, they can’t do that semantic conversion for the consumer.

Means, variances and cross terms have to change together

Let the cross-covariance between historical and current states be CCC, the current observation Jacobian HHH, the innovation covariance SSS and the innovation ν\nuν. When measurement noise is independent of the listed states, or the correlated pressure driver is already in the augmented state, history should get its own gain:

Khist=CHTS−1.K_{\mathrm{hist}}=CH^\mathsf{T}S^{-1}.Khist​=CHTS−1.

Then not only historical means but also historical variances and history–current cross terms must be updated. Attitude is injected by finite rotation, with the related covariance moved into the new tangent space. Changing only the quaternion, only the last position, or keeping only the cross block isn’t enough to get a consistent joint posterior.

This also doesn’t authorize the system to rewrite old outputs already published externally. Publication records stay as they were; the algorithm internally maintains a separate “current estimate of the history,” used for later correction, prediction and numerical checks. The two need clear names and data cutoffs, and a retrospective result can’t be passed off as the real-time output of the time just to draw a smooth route.

The jointly cut-off source still uses the original EKF linearization and conditional history updates; it doesn’t relinearize every old IMU, network and observation. Finite attitude injection doesn’t automatically give an exact fully nonlinear smoothed posterior either. What this fix does first is make the statistical objects consistent, then state clearly the local approximation it keeps.

After the fix: what really improved, and what it cost

In the stairs walk’s EN-only jointly cut-off source, once later observations correctly propagate revisions into history, the midpoint deviation RMS of unanchored half-second nodes falls from about 0.940 m to 0.028 m, and the maximum gap between position increments and trapezoidal velocity integration falls from about 10.23 m to about 0.15 m. That’s a state-consistency improvement from fixing what the source means, with no height clipping.

But with no pressure, the whole segment’s height still drifts down by about 46.05 m. The multi-meter sawtooth and the slow drift are two different problems: the former came mainly from drawing estimate revisions as physical motion, the latter from too little vertical observability or a mismatched motion model. Removing the sawtooth isn’t the same as controlling the drift.

Adding the same pressure and the two given exit regions gives a set of results that have to be read together:

There’s also a numerical trap that’s easy to waste time on. The EN-only source has very large vertical uncertainty, so the matrix scales differ enormously. The raw-coordinate gradient after a direct inverse looks sizable, but an independent reference using Cholesky, noise whitening and a thin SVD gives a whitened gradient relative norm of about 4.9×10−124.9\times10^{-12}4.9×10−12 and a state solution difference of about 2.8×10−92.8\times10^{-9}2.8×10−9. So before deciding a solver hasn’t converged, make sure the check itself is reliable at ill-conditioned scales. Don’t raise tolerances as soon as you see a large gradient, and don’t add diagonal jitter to make a factorization pass.

Part 4: Pressure inside the shared filter

Feeding pressure into the shared filter, so it shapes the future

Until now, pressure had mostly been added offline after the source was generated. That could fix saved segments, but the corrected height, velocity, attitude and related states never took part in later actual propagation. The next step was to make pressure a real update inside the native shared posterior:

  1. Raw pressure forms windows at exact physical times.
  2. A relative height measurement is built on the corresponding historical node.
  3. Related navigation states and the pressure memory are updated together.
  4. Later propagation, network inputs and candidate predictions use this revised state.
  5. Pressure already consumed is not added a second time as a “new in-graph factor.”

The last item is especially easy to forget. After moving pressure into the front end, adding the same batch of pressure residuals back to a source already corrected by pressure counts one observation twice. It may make curves fit pressure better and covariances smaller, but those surface improvements add no independent information.

Keeping pressure correlation without carrying infinite history

If every new pressure window extends the full pressure covariance matrix, compute and memory grow with the length of the walk. What we want to keep isn’t every old matrix itself, but the shared information that still affects the future.

This development model uses a finite moving-average noise expression:

ϵk=∑j=0128ajwk−j,wj∼N(0,1).\epsilon_k=\sum_{j=0}^{128}a_jw_{k-j},\qquad w_j\sim N(0,1).ϵk​=j=0∑128​aj​wk−j​,wj​∼N(0,1).

The 129 coefficients come from the biased empirical autocovariance of the original fixed pressure model’s early segment. It’s a finite-state implementation of the original model, not a freshly fitted AR model, and not a white-noise floor added to flatten height.

For the original covariance with the same zero extension, conditioning can continue with one shared reference variable plus a finite number of noise drivers that still affect the future. The implementation keeps a shared reference plus 128 drivers resident, 129 dimensions of pressure memory in total. When a new driver arrives it’s added first, and directions that no longer affect the future are released after consumption, so momentarily there’s one extra dimension.

That dimension isn’t the whole navigation system being 129-dimensional, nor 129 independent sensors. Navigation state, active targets, candidate branches, source responses and temporary solver workspace each take their own space. It only answers “how much state does this fixed pressure noise model need to keep?”

Concretely, write the shared reference as q=U0+ϵ0q=U_0+\epsilon_0q=U0​+ϵ0​ and later relative observations as di=Ui+ϵi−qd_i=U_i+\epsilon_i-qdi​=Ui​+ϵi​−q. So q isn’t just a fixed pressure value; it also carries the starting U’s uncertainty and its relationship with navigation state. The first window establishes that relationship and provides no extra independent height measurement. The noise already enters through the driver states, so an independent R can’t be wrapped around it again to add the same measurement noise twice.

The finite implementation also keeps the original model’s zero extension at long enough lags. That doesn’t mean real environmental drift necessarily disappears after the same time span. Covering the previous second with a median while binding it approximately to the window end is an observation-operator approximation; the roughly half-window support difference can’t be mechanically subtracted as a fixed sensor delay.

What really matters is that when releasing old nodes, the cross-covariance between pressure memory and navigation state can’t be thrown away along with them. Otherwise new pressure may still appear to enter the graph, having already lost the relationship “which past states it shares reference error with.”

The finite representation first has to match the reconstructed original covariance, then per-window updates and elimination are checked for consistency. In the stairs walk’s 99-window algebraic comparison, the first window establishes the reference and the following 98 provide information. Part 5: Back to flat floors, and into the runtime

Back on a flat floor, the full history overturned an early optimism

With real stairs restored, we had to go back to the flat-floor walk and check whether dropping learned U and switching to native pressure had brought new costs. Looking only at the first 129 pressure windows, one source’s height span was about 1.067 m, which looked more normal than before. Extending to the full A prefix changed the picture.

The original six radio fixes, Student-t ν4 with the original Q, finite start and heading model were all kept. At the same nine reference points, mean horizontal error dropped from about 2.557586 to 2.531981 m, with mean horizontal speed nearly unchanged. But the full-prefix U span grew from 0.585540 m to 2.116826 m. More importantly, before this Radio was even added, the new source already had a span of 2.117317 m.

So the new radio fit didn’t create this main rise and fall. Nor can it all be credited to “fusing pressure earlier,” because relative to the old A, the measurement choice also changed from learned XYZ to EN-only. Several changes happened at once, and the final two curves alone can’t isolate each one’s contribution.

That isn’t inherently wrong; the question is whether the change is compatible with the shared model and the actual trajectory.

Back in the runtime: why an old rejection blocked the right posterior

Even after the offline math was right, old logic in the runtime could still block things. A typical event happened near 82 s in the control walk. The raw TLIO rose about 1.068 m in half a second, the old Floor auxiliary graph’s Motion vertical envelope rejected the input, and the whole navigation run was terminated.

The problem was that native had already taken over the real shared navigation and already had a pressure-constrained posterior. The native input for that 82 s batch had been consumed, but the old gate blocked the flow before that window’s output preparation and pressure conditioning; the latest publication was still stuck at 81.5 s. The old auxiliary graph was looking at raw Motion that was still drifting. It no longer carried the final estimate of actual continuous U, yet it still had the power to veto the whole system.

The fix didn’t raise the original threshold or clip raw data. Only when preconditions hold (native has really taken over, pressure is working, the old floor–height coupling is off) is the old auxiliary edge’s vertical-envelope failure recorded as a diagnostic. Raw identity, timing and covariance checks, and the original gates for the old run mode, all stay in place.

The principle behind it is to let each check act on the object it has the right to judge. The raw TLIO anomaly needs to be kept, studied and fixed, but “the raw source looks bad” can’t be equated with “the current posterior, corrected by real observations, is invalid.” Conversely, a bounded posterior doesn’t mean the raw front end’s kinematics have been rescued.

Losing the original pressure window at startup causes a late, large correction

The runtime also exposed another kind of bug. Between neighboring publications at 72.0→72.5 s, height jumped about 9.899299 m, exactly when the pressure update count went from zero to one. On a plot it looks like pressure suddenly pulled things back ten meters, but the root cause was startup material not being inherited correctly.

The original pressure reference was at 52 s, and the scheduling object meant to receive it wasn’t created until 71 s; the old path, keeping only about twelve seconds of history, could no longer find the first window. One empty delivery also marked the receiver as started, so initialization wrongly used the current front. The system first published state without the original pressure properly connected, and then the late first pressure update produced the big jump.

The fix keeps a bounded cache of the original events and hands over the first window unchanged when the scheduling object is created, restoring the exact target, both clocks, the original median and the raw identity together. Grid points in between with no exact historical target only advance the noise drivers; no observations are fabricated. This changed neither the pressure formula nor the weights; it fixed the execution order of the first reference and the first valid consumption.

Two numbers with similar units need keeping apart: the old auxiliary graph’s 1.5 m/s vertical speed gate and the roughly 1.5 m after-the-fact physical check for same-floor walking are two different things. The latter was never used in place of the algorithm fixes above.

Part 6: Putting it together

How floor changes, continuous height and stair re-anchoring fit together

By this point, three responsibilities had finally become clear.

The first is Floor. It explains whether a legal floor transition happened and manages events, direction, landings and floor numbers. It’s responsible for half floors, turning back, continuous climbing and wrong activity labels.

The second is the continuous pressure posterior. It constrains the real relative change in U, keeps the shared reference and time correlation, and lets pressure corrections keep influencing later position, velocity, attitude and biases. It doesn’t reset every height to a floor plane just because Floor committed an integer.

The third is stair-exit position correction. It uses the confirmed entry–transition–exit relationship to add a region constraint on EN. A person inside the region shouldn’t be pulled toward its center. When several stairs can’t be told apart, candidates are kept; you can’t first pick the nearest stair by drifted position and then use that choice to prove the position is right.

These three chains share some inputs, so sources need recording. Pressure having helped recognize an arrival doesn’t mean the same pressure can then be used as a new, independent “exit confirmation probability.” Radio already used for stair disambiguation can’t be reused as an independent short factor either.

The continuous pressure reference and the Floor event reference need keeping apart too. One serves difference observations and the shared posterior; the other serves transition detection and re-arming events. A state machine resetting its working baseline for the next floor shouldn’t quietly swap every observation already in the continuous model over to a different reference.

The loop in this diagram that’s easiest to overlook is “keep needed correlations, then release old nodes.” An original node leaving the ten-second window doesn’t mean all the pressure information it carried can be deleted; but keeping information doesn’t mean storing every frame’s full matrix forever either. Finite memory and joint marginalization are exactly the mathematical interface between those two requirements.

Lessons worth reusing

Separate sensor changes, estimate updates and history revisions first

When a curve jumps, first ask what kind of curve it is. A raw pressure jump, the current position revised by one measurement, an old node rewritten by later data and a coordinate re-expression can all change the numbers on a plot, and each needs a different fix. The flat-floor walk’s three-way attribution table shows the final valley can’t be blamed directly on one pressure update at that time; the stairs walk’s half-second spikes show that publications with different cutoffs can’t be stitched into one joint physical history.

Numerical convergence is a necessary check, not a substitute for physical sense

The stairs walk converged, yet compromised a +2.309 m pressure rise down to about −0.199 m. More iterations were pointless, because the error was already in the objective. Conversely, crude gradient checks on ill-conditioned matrices can falsely report non-convergence. Check against a stable reference first, then decide whether to change the solver or the model.

A correct covariance and a credible covariance are two different things

Keeping the shared reference, Motion source and history cross terms makes the information computation consistent. Motion giving only a 0.0841 m standard deviation for a local rise while disagreeing with pressure by about 2.49 m exposes model mismatch or uncovered error. “The matrix matches the reference to machine precision” can’t vouch for that kind of statistical credibility.

Not every bad result is barometer noise

A whole route tilted into a ramp, weak learned U response, sawtooth from frozen history, an old auxiliary graph wrongly vetoing the current posterior: none of these is solved by low-passing pressure. Filtering can reduce some high-frequency noise, and it can also add latency and change real rises and falls. Before adopting it, you need to know what you’re trying to remove.

Flatter on the same floor isn’t necessarily better

Pinning U to the floor plane eliminates every rise and fall, including real stairs. Worse local solutions can be just as flat. Look at flat floors, stairs, horizontal shape and velocity together, and don’t use output range as a model selector. Physical common sense raises the questions; real measurements and explainable models answer them.

Keep the negative results, so you know what not to redo

After the joint-cutoff fix, horizontal error regressed for a while; native pressure feedback didn’t remove the remaining roughly 0.73 m mismatch; the millimeter-level differences from re-running the network aren’t worth exaggerating; the flat-floor walk’s full prefix overturned the optimistic reading of the short early segment. All of these belong next to the successes, or a later maintainer can easily retry explanations the evidence has already ruled out.

One observation carries information once; moving modules doesn’t add evidence

After moving pressure from the offline graph into native, the original same-batch pressure factors have to be removed. Same-source information from AHRS, IMU and TLIO can’t be added again under another name either. If source records don’t enter the actual computation and live only in logs, they still can’t prevent overconfidence.

Algorithm boundedness, process boundedness and whole-window real time have to be measured separately

129 dimensions of pressure memory show that a specific noise model can be maintained finitely; the 363/372-dimension comparison shows that one shared computation can release old history. Neither covers the Python process, background candidates, databases, IPC and whole-window scheduling. A control run finishing completely didn’t make the 500 ms budget happen on its own.

Where this stands, and what would be worth verifying next

Based on the saved original reports, we can confirm the following. The barometer has gone from “only feeding trends to the floor state machine” to a measurement that enters the shared navigation posterior, constrains continuous height and keeps its influence on the future. The flattening of real stairs was recovered in the selected stairs-walk comparisons. With historical means, variances and correlations updated together, non-physical half-second sawtooth dropped sharply. And the numerical implementation of finite pressure memory with old-node release has an independent reference.

It’s equally clear that there isn’t enough evidence yet to claim the pressure model is calibrated across logs. The flat-floor walk’s full prefix still shows same-floor rise and fall, the full control run still has a span of about 2.15 m, and these segments can’t stand in for the long-term joint behavior of every correction chain or accuracy on the official long logs.

That last item can’t be skipped. Short segments can show a mechanism works under specified conditions, but they can’t cover long slow drift, reference baseline changes, repeated floor changes, source release and rollback. Once the local algorithm and the whole-window runtime are finished, the full official long logs still have to be run on the frozen configuration.

Looking back on this stretch of development, the deepest change wasn’t the barometer formula getting more complicated; it was getting stricter about what the word “height” means. It needs a defined physical coordinate, measurement sources, a shared reference, a data cutoff and uncertainty. It has to allow real climbs, and it has to refuse to draw estimate revisions as a person’s motion. Only when all of those agree can the pressure curve, the floor labels and the 3D trajectory tell one credible story of a walk.

A floor label says which level you’re on; height is only trustworthy once you know what reference, what data and what history it was computed from.

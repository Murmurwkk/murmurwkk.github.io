Ignoring Cross-Covariance Turned a Pause into a 7 m/s Sprint

Same observations, two answers: with the full common source a standing person moves at 0.18 m/s; treat motion edges as independent and it's 7 m/s. The mechanism, the ablation and the diagnostics.

更新于 2026/11/6 · 11 个章节
#common covariance#Motion#Kalman update#navigation diagnostics#case study

Somewhere around the five-minute mark of a flat-floor walking log (T31), the person holding the phone pauses. Differencing consecutive estimated positions gives a horizontal speed of about 0.18 m/s, which is what you’d expect from someone standing around. The velocity stored inside the estimator’s state for the same stretch says 7 m/s. That’s a brisk sprint.

On a map you’d never notice. The two trajectories overlap almost perfectly. The contradiction only exists inside the joint state, where position and velocity are supposed to agree with each other and very much don’t.

What makes this case worth a full post is how controlled it is. The 7 m/s and the 0.18 m/s come from the same observations: 17 Radio position hypotheses and 346 pressure differences, with yaw fixed in advance. The only thing that differs is the covariance those observations use to reach back into the trajectory history. With the full common source, the mean pause speed is 0.181593 m/s. Treat the relative Motion edges as independent noise instead, and it becomes 6.978458 m/s.

In this post I’ll build up the mechanism from a two-number example, show where the cross-covariance in a trajectory comes from and how one common compression destroys it, and then walk through a six-arm ablation on that walk that isolates the effect. Along the way we’ll compute the velocity response without the full matrix, and see why “the speeds match” is weaker evidence than it sounds.

Setting the stage

The estimator in question fuses three kinds of evidence on a phone:

  • Motion: a learned inertial front end that turns IMU data into relative motion over short windows, with an associated covariance.
  • Radio: WiFi-based position hypotheses, each with its own covariance QQQ.
  • Pressure: barometer differences, which share a common reference pressure.

The state is a history of position, velocity and attitude at half-second nodes. Logs like this one come with sparse POSI reference points, ground-truth positions used here only for evaluation, never for fitting. The question in this post is narrow: given a fixed set of observations, how does the representation of the Motion history’s uncertainty change what those observations do to velocity?

A two-number primer on cross-covariance

Let’s start with the smallest example that shows the effect. Position and velocity both have variance 1, and their cross-covariance is 0.8. A position measurement arrives with innovation 2 and noise variance 1.

  • Position moves by 2/(1+1)=12/(1+1)=12/(1+1)=1.
  • Velocity moves by 0.8×2/(1+1)=0.80.8\times2/(1+1)=0.80.8×2/(1+1)=0.8.

Now zero out the cross term. Position still moves by 1, but velocity doesn’t move at all. Same measurement, same marginals, completely different velocity update.

Figure 1: A constructed, dimensionless example. Both ellipses have the same position and velocity marginals but different joint relationships; the red arrows are the change in conditional mean. Not fitted to the walk.

The general version is the familiar Kalman gain, split into blocks. With

x=[pv],P=[PppPpvPvpPvv],x=\begin{bmatrix}p\\v\end{bmatrix}, \qquad P= \begin{bmatrix} P_{pp}&P_{pv}\\ P_{vp}&P_{vv} \end{bmatrix},x=[pv​],P=[Ppp​Pvp​​Ppv​Pvv​​],

a position observation z=Hp+ϵz=Hp+\epsilonz=Hp+ϵ with noise covariance RRR (independent of the pre-update state error) and innovation r=z−Hp^r=z-H\hat pr=z−Hp^​ gives

S=HPppH⊤+R,S=HP_{pp}H^\top+R,S=HPpp​H⊤+R, δp=PppH⊤S−1r,δv=PvpH⊤S−1r.\delta p=P_{pp}H^\top S^{-1}r, \qquad \delta v=P_{vp}H^\top S^{-1}r.δp=Ppp​H⊤S−1r,δv=Pvp​H⊤S−1r.

Velocity appears nowhere in the measurement function, and yet PvpP_{vp}Pvp​ hands it a share of the correction. That’s a feature. If the end position is off, it’s usually because a velocity error got integrated, so a filter that fixes position while leaving velocity untouched is often the wrong one.

It’s also where things go wrong. If the system’s PvpP_{vp}Pvp​ doesn’t match the real common error, a position update can push velocity to the wrong order of magnitude, even though the observation contains no velocity information at all. And none of the usual checks will see it: position residuals look fine, position marginal variances look fine, and each factor’s Jacobian is correct.

📝 Note: These formulas assume the measurement noise is independent of the state error. If the radio labels themselves were derived from the same inertial trajectory, that correlation would need its own term. The experiment below holds the measurement model fixed and only varies the Motion representation, so it makes no claim that Radio noise is independent.

Where cross-covariance comes from in a trajectory

So where does PvpP_{vp}Pvp​, and more generally the cross-time covariance, come from? Stack the historical states produced by the filter:

X=[x0⊤x1⊤⋯xn⊤]⊤,PX=Cov⁡(X).X=\begin{bmatrix}x_0^\top&x_1^\top&\cdots&x_n^\top\end{bmatrix}^\top, \qquad P_X=\operatorname{Cov}(X).X=[x0⊤​​x1⊤​​⋯​xn⊤​​]⊤,PX​=Cov(X).

These states share a lot: the initial state, inertial noise, biases, overlapping learning windows, later updates that touched several of them at once. So PXP_XPX​ is far from block-diagonal. Even for two scalar positions,

Var⁡(pj−pi)=Pjj+Pii−Pji−Pij.\operatorname{Var}(p_j-p_i) =P_{jj}+P_{ii}-P_{ji}-P_{ij}.Var(pj​−pi​)=Pjj​+Pii​−Pji​−Pij​.

The cross terms aren’t just a fudge factor that makes variances bigger. A common translation error cancels in the difference; other correlation patterns do something else entirely. Throwing them away isn’t “conservative” in any consistent direction.

The compression that breaks it

A very common move is to re-express the history as “a starting point plus relative edges”:

Y=TX.Y=T X.Y=TX.

For a fixed linearization and invertible TTT, the honest covariance of the new coordinates is

PY=TPXT⊤.P_Y=T P_X T^\top.PY​=TPX​T⊤.

Keep all of PYP_YPY​ and nothing has changed; it’s just new notation. The trouble starts when you keep only the diagonal blocks, the start covariance and each edge’s own covariance, and zero out everything between them:

P~Y=blockdiag⁡(Px0,Q1,…,Qn)\widetilde P_Y =\operatorname{blockdiag}(P_{x_0},Q_1,\ldots,Q_n)PY​=blockdiag(Px0​​,Q1​,…,Qn​)

This is no longer a change of variables. It’s a different statistical model. Every QiQ_iQi​ can be the correct projection of its two end-points and the model can still be wrong, because nothing about those projections says the edge errors are independent of each other.

Here’s the simplest way to see it. Let every position share a bias: pi=b+eip_i=b+e_ipi​=b+ei​. Differencing kills bbb, which sounds like independence. But neighboring differences pi+1−pip_{i+1}-p_ipi+1​−pi​ and pi−pi−1p_i-p_{i-1}pi​−pi−1​ both contain eie_iei​. So the absolute states share an error and the relative edges are correlated with each other, at the same time. Stare at any single edge’s marginal and you’ll see neither.

The experiment: three ways to represent one history

To isolate the effect of that compression, the flat-floor diagnostic builds three representations of the same Motion history, from the same source linearization, the same initial covariance and the same nominal mean:

  1. Full common source. Use the needed blocks of the full cross-time covariance.
  2. Adjacent conditional chain. Derive a conditional transition between neighbors from their joint blocks. This keeps neighbor statistics but makes a Markov approximation that drops non-adjacent conditional dependence.
  3. Independent relative edges. Keep the start covariance and each edge’s projected covariance, then treat the edges as independent process noise. This is the old compression.

The adjacent chain uses, at each step,

Fi=Pi+1,iPii−1,Qi=Pi+1,i+1−FiPi,i+1.F_i=P_{i+1,i}P_{ii}^{-1}, \qquad Q_i=P_{i+1,i+1}-F_iP_{i,i+1}.Fi​=Pi+1,i​Pii−1​,Qi​=Pi+1,i+1​−Fi​Pi,i+1​.

That matches the adjacent marginals by construction, but it doesn’t make the original history first-order Markov. Keep that in mind when it performs well below.

Each representation is run under two fixed headings: the original heading of about −73.644°, and about −36.742° taken from a saved conditional Q solution. Yaw isn’t optimized and POSI isn’t used to choose it. Everything else is pinned:

The result: two orders of magnitude

The pause, chosen from behavior and reference records, runs from 302.024 to 337.736 s and contains 71 states. There’s no zero-velocity constraint in the optimization and no per-step velocity ground truth. We compare two speeds. The mean state speed is

sˉv=1N∑ivE,i2+vN,i2.\bar s_v=\frac1N\sum_i\sqrt{v_{E,i}^2+v_{N,i}^2}.sˉv​=N1​i∑​vE,i2​+vN,i2​​.

The mean position-difference speed uses neighboring states with both ends inside the interval:

di=pi+1−piti+1−ti,sˉp=1N−1∑i∥di,EN∥.d_i=\frac{p_{i+1}-p_i}{t_{i+1}-t_i}, \qquad \bar s_p=\frac1{N-1}\sum_i\|d_{i,EN}\|.di​=ti+1​−ti​pi+1​−pi​​,sˉp​=N−11​i∑​∥di,EN​∥.

Both are computed from the actual half-second samples inside the interval, with nothing stitched in from outside.

Under the hood: the response without the giant matrix

How do you compute this without building a 6237×6237 update every time? Only the source coordinates touched by observations matter. Call that subset ZZZ and define

K=Cov⁡(Z),C=Cov⁡(X,Z).K=\operatorname{Cov}(Z),\qquad C=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z).K=Cov(Z),C=Cov(X,Z).

The external observations are linear in the source perturbation and an independent physical start bbb with covariance BBB:

r=MδZ+Hbδb+ϵ,r=M\delta Z+H_b\delta b+\epsilon,r=MδZ+Hb​δb+ϵ,

which gives

S=MKM⊤+HbBHb⊤+R,S=MKM^\top+H_bBH_b^\top+R,S=MKM⊤+Hb​BHb⊤​+R, δX=CM⊤S−1r,δb=BHb⊤S−1r.\delta X=CM^\top S^{-1}r,\qquad \delta b=BH_b^\top S^{-1}r.δX=CM⊤S−1r,δb=BHb⊤​S−1r.

The velocity rows of δX\delta XδX are the response of the entire velocity history to this batch of observations. Notice the two ingredients. You need KKK, the covariance among observed coordinates, and you need CCC, the cross block between every state and those coordinates. Storing position marginals at the observed points isn’t enough to recover the velocity response.

The core of the historical implementation is exactly this:

S = M @ K @ M.T + H_b @ B @ H_b.T + R
alpha = solve(S, innovation)
delta_source = C @ (M.T @ alpha)
delta_anchor = B @ (H_b.T @ alpha)

It uses a Cholesky factorization rather than adding jitter to hide inconsistencies, and it keeps the pressure noise matrix correlated, because several differences share the same reference pressure.

The three representations differ only in the common structure encoded in KKK and CCC. For fitting positions, they can all produce similar slow trajectories. For the velocity cross-response, they can differ wildly. That’s why plotting the EN route alone hides this class of bug so well.

Sanity check on a single WiFi hypothesis

It’s reassuring to watch the same formula behave sensibly in a smaller setting. A separate saved diagnostic conditions on just one Gaussian position hypothesis, at 322 s or 370 s, which makes it easy to inspect PvpP_{vp}Pvp​ at each historical state.

At 322 s the recorded innovation and its covariance are about

r=[15.9603−33.2355] m,S=[2.56470−0.44205−0.442054.73191] m2.r=\begin{bmatrix}15.9603\\-33.2355\end{bmatrix}\text{ m}, \qquad S= \begin{bmatrix} 2.56470&-0.44205\\ -0.44205&4.73191 \end{bmatrix}\text{ m}^2.r=[15.9603−33.2355​] m,S=[2.56470−0.44205​−0.442054.73191​] m2.

That’s a huge innovation, tens of meters. Yet the target state’s velocity changes by only

δv=[0.01003−0.01283−0.00126] m/s.\delta v= \begin{bmatrix} 0.01003\\-0.01283\\-0.00126 \end{bmatrix}\text{ m/s}.δv=​0.01003−0.01283−0.00126​​ m/s.

The hypothesis has an NIS of about 298.93, so nobody should read this as an argument for accepting it. The point is the scale: a tens-of-meters position innovation produces a centimeters-per-second velocity response, because the cross-covariance says it should. The size of the innovation alone doesn’t decide the response; PvpP_{vp}Pvp​ does.

Recomputing over the saved time offsets:

alpha = np.linalg.solve(innovation_covariance, innovation)
delta_v = velocity_position_cross_covariance @ alpha

matches the historical vectors to within 2.78×10−172.78\times10^{-17}2.78×10−17 m/s per component.

Don’t combine this with the 6.98 m/s, though. That number comes from the joint Radio-plus-pressure update; this one is a single position hypothesis. Different observation sets, different questions.

Matching speeds isn’t matching velocities

You might object that position differences and state velocity shouldn’t match exactly anyway. That’s true. Estimated positions get revised by every update. If pj−p_j^-pj−​ and pj+p_j^+pj+​ are the position before and after an update,

pn+−p0+=∑j=1n(pj−−pj−1+)+∑j=1n(pj+−pj−).p_n^+-p_0^+ =\sum_{j=1}^n(p_j^--p_{j-1}^+) +\sum_{j=1}^n(p_j^+-p_j^-).pn+​−p0+​=j=1∑n​(pj−​−pj−1+​)+j=1∑n​(pj+​−pj−​).

The first sum is displacement from propagation; the second is the accumulated revisions. Differencing saved positions mixes both, so it isn’t integrated physical velocity. And a smoother’s current clone of a past state may not be the version that was saved back then.

So small disagreements are expected. A sustained factor of almost 40, with matched control arms and a directly decomposed update response, is not something revisions can explain. It points straight at the representation that changed.

There’s a subtler trap in the other direction, too: similar speeds don’t mean similar velocity vectors. In the −36.742° full-source arm, the two mean speeds are 0.181593 and 0.182647, which look like agreement. But the mean magnitude of the vector difference (using midpoint velocities between neighbors) is about 0.220797 m/s, larger than either speed. For the independent-edge arm, it’s about 7.006465 m/s. Comparing magnitudes would have hidden the first discrepancy entirely, which is why the vector difference is the number worth keeping.

What this does and doesn’t show

The ablation is tightly scoped, so it’s worth being explicit:

  • It shows that, under identical inputs, replacing the full common source with independent relative edges produces the extreme velocity response, and that the response can be recomputed from the saved matrices.
  • It doesn’t show that the full-source pause estimate is accurate. There’s no velocity ground truth, and the vector difference above is still 0.22 m/s.
  • The adjacent conditional chain is close here, but it drops non-adjacent dependence; whether that holds on other trajectories needs its own test.
  • Everything is conditioned on the selected position hypotheses. Full multimodal and NULL decisions, the calibration of the full matrix itself and continuous-run behavior are all untested.
  • The front-end network wasn’t rerun and the original covariance wasn’t recalibrated; this reproduces the conditional updates of a frozen error process.

Conclusion

Let’s recap. A position factor can and should change velocity, through the position–velocity cross-covariance. In a trajectory, that cross-covariance comes from errors shared across time. Compressing the history into independent relative edges keeps each edge’s variance but throws away exactly those shared errors, and in the flat-floor walk that single change turned a 0.18 m/s pause into 7 m/s, with every observation held fixed.

If you build estimators like this, I’d keep two diagnostics around permanently: the velocity vector before and after each update, with its response, and the cross block from the source coordinates to the observed ones. Route plots, position marginals and observation Jacobians all look fine while this bug is happening.

A trajectory can look right on the map and still be wrong inside the state, and the cross-covariance is where you find out.
